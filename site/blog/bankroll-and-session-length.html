Bankroll and session length: how far a fixed stack actually goes

The house edge gives an answer to this question, and the answer is wrong by a factor of about seven. How long a stack lasts is decided by the ratio between the stack and the bet, and almost nothing else.

The obvious calculation is a division. On 9/6 Jacks or Better the return is 99.54%, so each coin staked costs 0.0046 coins. Five coins a hand makes that 0.023 coins per hand. A 1,000-coin stack divided by 0.023 is 43,832 hands — something like 110 hours at a brisk pace. On that arithmetic a modest stack is effectively bottomless.

Nobody's stack behaves like that. Deal out the same 1,000 coins hand by hand, five at a time, with correct play throughout, and half the time it is gone inside 6,540 hands. The division is not wrong about the average; it is answering a different question. The house edge describes where the money ends up after a million hands. Session length is decided by variance, which is roughly nine hundred and fifty times larger per hand.

Stacks are measured in bets, not in coins

The useful unit is the bet. A 1,000-coin stack at five coins a hand and a 10,000-coin stack at fifty are the same stack: 200 bets. Both last the same number of hands, which is why the only bankroll question worth asking is how many bets deep you are.

Read the first row and the last together. Twenty bets is not a short session, it is a coin flip you lose five times out of six: the median is 194 hands, around half an hour. Fifty bets gets you to roughly a thousand hands half the time. Two hundred bets will almost certainly see out an evening and still has a one-in-three chance of surviving ten thousand hands. The curve is steep at the bottom and flattens fast, which means the first hundred bets of depth buy far more time than the second hundred.

The machine you play is not the machine on the paytable

Here is why the division fails so badly. That 99.54% includes the royal flush, which pays 800 per coin and arrives about once in 40,400 hands. Strike that one row out and the return falls to 97.56% — a figure covered at more length in the royal's share of the return.

For any session you will actually sit through, striking it out is the honest assumption. So the drain rate to plan against is not 0.023 coins a hand but 0.122 — at five coins a hand and 400 hands an hour, 49 coins an hour rather than 9. That is five times the nominal cost, and it is still only the average; it says nothing about the spread around it.

This is the arithmetic behind the old rule of thumb that a Jacks or Better bankroll should be around 250 bets, or three royals' worth at five coins. It is not superstition about the big hand. It is the recognition that the royal is the only thing holding the return above 99%, that you will almost certainly not be dealt one, and that the stack therefore has to survive a 97.6% machine for as long as you intend to play.

What the drain feels like

Something pays on 45.5% of hands, but 21.5 points of that is a high pair, which hands the stake straight back. You finish a hand genuinely ahead 24% of the time. Three hands in four take coins off the stack or leave it level, and that is what the slow decline is made of.

Runs of nothing are ordinary rather than remarkable. A hand pays nothing 54.5% of the time, so five blanks in a row turn up about once every 21 hands, ten in a row about once every 429. Widen it to hands that do not leave you ahead and ten of those in succession arrive about once every 16 hands. A stretch of twenty dead hands — about once in 184,000 — is the kind of thing that feels like a broken machine and is simply the deck doing what it does. None of it is information about the next deal.

The bet is the only dial

Return, strategy and hand frequencies are all fixed once you have chosen the paytable. Depth in bets is the one thing left under your control, and it moves inversely with the stake: halve the bet and you double the hands. Multi-hand works the other way, multiplying the stake per deal and dividing your depth by the number of lines.

At a casino machine there is a catch, which is why the dial is not free. The royal only pays 800 per coin at the full five-coin bet; short-coin it pays 250. Losing 550 per coin on a hand that lands once in 40,400 costs about 1.4 points of return before any strategy adjustment, which is three times the entire house edge. Dropping to one coin buys five times the hands and turns a 99.5% machine into something close to 98.2%. Betting max is not optional on those machines, so the only honest way to get more hands out of a fixed stack is a lower-denomination machine, not a smaller bet on the same one.

Two things follow for anyone planning a session. Convert the stack into bets before anything else, and check it against the table above rather than against the house edge. And set the stopping point in hands or in minutes, not in coins — a stack that is still there is not evidence of a hot machine, and one that has gone early is not evidence of a cold one.
