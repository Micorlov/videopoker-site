The name is misleading in a useful way. Video poker is five-card draw dealt from an ordinary 52-card deck, with the hand rankings everyone already knows. A table player can sit down and never misread a hand. They will still play it badly, because the one structural fact — there is nobody on the other side of it — changes what the cards are for.

Your hand is measured against a chart, not against someone else's

At a table, hand strength is relative. You win the pot by holding something better than the player who calls you, and how much better is irrelevant. At a machine your hand is compared against a fixed list of payouts, and nothing else about it matters.

The sharpest illustration is the bottom of the chart. A pair of tens beats a pair of nines every time at a table. On a Jacks or Better machine a pair of tens and a pair of nines are worth exactly the same thing: nothing. The threshold row draws a line through the ranks, and below it the deck has no gradations at all. Rank also stops being a tiebreak and becomes a payout. Four deuces and four aces both pay 25 per coin on 9/6 Jacks or Better; on Double Bonus the aces pay 160 and four 5s through kings pay 50. Same category, three times the money, and at a table the distinction does not exist.

It runs the other way at the top, too. A royal flush beats a seven-high straight flush at a table, and both of them simply win the pot. On 9/6 rows with five coins in, the royal pays 800 per coin and the straight flush pays 50 — sixteen times as much for a hand that, at a table, would often have won the same money.

There is no fold, so every deal gets played

Most of what a disciplined table player does is decline to play. The hand is dealt, it is not worth the money, and it costs nothing to put it down. At a machine you have already paid when the cards appear, and there is no way to give the hand back.

Half of all deals arrive with no made hand in them. At a table that is a fold and a two-second decision. At a machine it is a choice between 32 possible holds — every subset of five cards, including all of them and none of them — and the right one is worth real money. Even the worst case pays something back: throw all five away on 9/6 rows and the draw returns 0.3224 coins per coin staked. A folded hand at a table returns zero and costs zero. A junk hand at a machine returns about a third of your stake, and the skill is in not making it less than that.

One decision, with nothing hidden

A hand of table poker is a sequence of betting rounds in which you are guessing at cards you cannot see and trying to control what other people guess about yours. Position, bet sizing, bluffing and reading all exist because there is an opponent who can be made to fold a better hand.

A machine cannot fold. It has no cards of its own, makes no decision, and does not care what you represent. So the bluffing half of poker is not merely weaker at a machine — it has nothing to attach to. What is left is one decision per hand, taken with complete information about your own five cards.

Which is why the game is solved and table poker is not. For each of the 2,598,960 possible deals there is one hold with the highest expected value, and perfect play is nothing more than knowing that list. 9/6 Jacks or Better returns 99.54% to a player who plays it. No equivalent number exists for a poker table, because your expectation there depends on who else sat down.

The cost arrives in a different place

At a table you are playing against the other players, and the house takes a rake out of the pot or charges for the seat. Your edge, if you have one, comes from them.

At a machine there are no other players to take money from, so the entire cost of the game sits in the paytable. It is concentrated, too: the full house and flush rows alone separate 9/6 Jacks or Better at 99.54% from the 8/5 version at about 97.3%. Same deck, same rankings, same correct strategy, two rows of small print, and 2.2 points of difference. Nothing at a poker table can be read off in two numbers like that, and nothing at a machine matters more. Read the chart before you play it.

What does carry over

Counting outs transfers intact. Eight cards complete an open-ended four-card straight and four complete a gutshot, and that is true at a table and at a machine alike. What changes is the comparison you make afterwards. At a table you weigh the count against the pot and the player who bet into you. At a machine you weigh it against the paytable, and the comparison has exactly one answer — on 9/6 rows the outside draw is worth 0.68 coins and the gutshot 0.34, every time, forever.

One table habit is worth unlearning deliberately. A kicker decides who wins a split hand at a table, so keeping an ace next to your pair is sensible there. At a machine the pair already pays or it does not, and the kicker simply costs you a card off the draw — about 0.12 coins on a high pair and 0.60 on two pair, and never once a gain. Carrying it over is the single most common mistake a table player makes on a machine.

The last difference is about how the two games feel. A pair of jacks on 9/6 is worth 1.5365 coins per coin and a low pair 0.8237. Both are, by table standards, nothing hands. At a machine they are the bulk of what you play, and the variance you actually notice is mostly the gap between them.
