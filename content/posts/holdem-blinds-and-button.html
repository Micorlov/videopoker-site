Hold'em has no ante in its basic form. Instead, two players put chips in before they see their cards, and the seat that does it moves one place round the table after every hand. If nobody paid anything, the best play would be to fold everything but aces. The forced bets give everyone a reason to contest the pot.

The button and the two blinds

A dealer button marks the nominal dealer and moves one seat clockwise after each hand. The two seats immediately clockwise from the button post the blinds:

  1. Small blind (SB): the first seat left of the button, posting half a big blind.
  2. Big blind (BB): the next seat, posting one full big blind.

So before anyone has looked at a card the pot holds 0.5 + 1 = 1.5 big blinds. That is the prize everyone is playing for.

Who acts when

The order differs before the flop and after it. Before the flop, the first player to act is the one left of the big blind, called under the gun (UTG). Action goes clockwise, so the button is third from last, then the small blind, and the big blind speaks last. After the flop, the turn and the river, the first player still in the hand who is left of the button acts first, so the small blind (if still in) leads and the button acts last.

The blinds act last before the flop but first after it. What that order is worth is covered in the position post; here the point is only the order.

The big blind's option

The big blind has already bet, but they are the last to act before the flop. If every player folds or merely calls the 1 big blind, the action returns to them with nothing to call. They can check and see the flop, or raise. This is the big blind's option. The betting round does not close until they have used it. If someone has raised, there is no option, and the big blind faces an ordinary decision: fold, call or re-raise.

The small blind has no such option. To stay in against a limp they must add 0.5 to match the 1 big blind, and they act first after the flop.

The heads-up exception

With two players the usual layout breaks down, so heads-up play changes it. The button posts the small blind, the other player posts the big blind, and the button acts first before the flop. After the flop the order reverses: the big blind acts first and the button acts last. The button is therefore last to act after the flop in every format, and in a heads-up game it also begins the betting.

What the blinds cost

A full trip of the button round the table is an orbit. In one orbit you pay the small blind once and the big blind once, 1.5 big blinds in all, however many players are seated. What changes is how many hands that is spread across.

A player with 100 big blinds who never plays a hand would be down 1.5 each orbit, so the stack lasts 100 ÷ 1.5 = 66.7 orbits. That is 600 hands nine-handed and about 133 heads-up. This is the pressure: sitting still has a price, and it is higher the fewer players share it.

Why the pot is worth fighting for

Suppose everyone folds to you and you raise to 2.5 big blinds. You risk 2.5 to win the 1.5 already in the middle. Ignoring your chance of winning when called, the raise breaks even when the others fold often enough that f × 1.5 = (1 − f) × 2.5. That gives f = 2.5 ÷ 4 = 62.5%. A raise to 3 needs 3 ÷ 4.5 = 66.7%.

Anyone who calls has some chance of winning, so this is a ceiling on what folds must deliver, not a promise. But the fewer opponents left to act, the likelier they all fold, which is why late seats open more hands.

The blinds get a discount on the price. Say a player raises to 3 big blinds and the small blind folds. The big blind has 1 in and needs 2 more. The pot after the call is 3 + 0.5 + 3 = 6.5, so the call needs 2 ÷ 6.5 = 30.8%. A player with nothing posted would need 3 ÷ 7.5 = 40% against the same raise. The method is the one in the pot odds post. A cheap price is not a good hand, though, and the big blind acts early on every later street.

The rule in one line: the blinds go last before the flop and first after it, and the button, which pays nothing, acts last on every street after the flop.
