A cue ball in American pool is 2¼ inches, or 57.15 mm, across. Its radius is therefore about 28.6 mm. The tip of the cue can only push on a small part of that surface with any control, and the edge of that part is where miscues begin. Once you know where it sits, most advice about chalk, spin and the awkward shots near the rail starts to make sense.

Why the tip slips

When the tip meets the ball at the exact centre, the push goes straight into the ball. Move the contact point away from centre and the surface tilts away from the line of the cue. The push splits in two: one part presses into the ball, the other tries to slide the tip across the surface. Friction between chalked leather and the ball resists the sliding part. While friction is enough, the tip grips and the ball leaves cleanly. When it is not, the tip skids off, which is a miscue.

The further out you strike, the bigger the sideways share becomes. The commonly quoted practical limit is about half the ball's radius from centre, roughly 14 mm on a standard cue ball. Treat that as an approximation: it depends on the tip, the chalk and the cleanliness of the ball, and players disagree about the last millimetre or two. It is still a good working figure, and it gives a striking zone that is a disc about 28 mm across inside a ball 57 mm across.

What chalk and the tip actually do

Chalk does not make the tip stick like glue. It raises the friction between leather and ball, which pushes the slip point a little further from centre. That is why a chalked tip can put more side spin on the ball than a bare one, and why a bare tip makes even modest side spin risky. Chalk wears off with every shot, so the habit of chalking before each stroke is a way of keeping the limit where you expect it.

The tip matters in the same way. A tip that has glazed and hardened grips less, and a tip shaped too flat or too pointed changes how much leather meets the ball. A clean ball helps too, because dirt on the surface lowers friction just as surely as a worn tip does.

None of this makes extreme spin comfortable. Spin comes from how far off centre you strike, and the zone ends at 14 mm. Asking for spin at 13 mm, one millimetre inside the limit, leaves almost no margin for a stroke that wanders a fraction. Cue-ball spin explained covers where on that disc each type of spin lives. Massé: the elevated cue

A massé is played with the cue raised steeply, sometimes close to vertical, striking the ball well off centre so that it curves instead of running straight. A level cue can still put side spin on the ball, but it travels in a straight line at first and the spin only shows up as a gentle change of direction after contact. With an elevated cue the downward part of the stroke drives the ball into the cloth while side spin grips it sideways, and the path bends.

The common explanation is that the limit still applies, measured from the cue's line through the centre of the ball. Raising the cue does not give extra grip. What it changes is how much of the spin turns into curve. This is the commonly given account and not a quoted rule, and exact figures vary by cloth and cue. The upshot is the same either way: with a steep cue you are working within the same 14 mm, and the margin for error does not improve. It is why massé looks hard and is hard.

That is also why extreme-spin shots are best treated as a last option. They are a game of millimetres played at the edge of a grip you cannot see.

What a miscue costs under the rules

The common understanding under the World Pool-Billiard Association general rules is that a miscue is not a foul in itself. The shot is played as it lies: whatever the cue ball does counts, and the ordinary rules decide the result. If the ball still hits a legal object ball first and something reaches a cushion or a pocket, nothing is wrong. If it misses the object ball, goes in a pocket or hits the wrong ball first, that is a foul for the usual reason, and your opponent gets ball in hand.

So the real cost is rarely a penalty. It is the loss of the shot you planned: the object ball goes somewhere else, the cue ball lands in the wrong place, and the position you were building falls apart. A related case is worth knowing, because it is a foul. If the tip contacts the cue ball more than once during one stroke, that is a double hit and a foul, and a slip followed by a second touch can fall into that category. Check the specific rule set you play under before relying on this.

What to do about it

  1. Strike inside the limit. If the shot needs spin near the edge of the zone, ask whether a shot with less spin gets the same result.
  2. Chalk before each shot, lightly and evenly, and keep the tip scuffed rather than glazed.
  3. Accept less spin than you think you need. The extra millimetres seldom change where the cue ball goes as much as they raise the chance of a miscue.
  4. Keep the stroke straight, with a smooth finish. A wandering stroke eats the margin before the tip arrives.
