The first sixty feet of a quarter mile are where most of the run is won or lost, and they are mostly a question of grip. An engine can make more force than the tyres can pass to the road, so extra revs at the start do not always help. This post works through one example car; the figures are illustrative, not those of any car in a game.

The tyres set the ceiling

The most force a tyre can put into the road is the friction coefficient multiplied by the load pressing it down: F = μ × N. Newton's second law says F = m × a, and N for a car with all its weight on the driven wheels is m × g, so the mass cancels:

maximum acceleration ≈ μ × g

A grippy slick has a coefficient of around 1, so the ceiling is roughly one g, or 32.2 ft/s². At constant acceleration the distance covered is ½ × a × t², which gives t = √(2d ÷ a). For 60 feet at 1 g that is √(120 ÷ 32.17) = 1.93 s. A rear-drive car cannot reach it, for the reason in the next section.

Weight moves to the back

A rear-drive car does not have all its weight on the driven wheels. Our example weighs 3,000 lb with 50% on the rear axle, so 1,500 lb presses on the driven tyres at rest. Acceleration shifts load backwards, by an amount equal to the weight times the acceleration (in g) times the centre-of-gravity height divided by the wheelbase. With a centre of gravity 20 inches up and a wheelbase of 100 inches, each 1 g of acceleration moves 3,000 × 1 × 20 ÷ 100 = 600 lb.

With μ = 1.0, the rear tyres can push with a force of 1.0 × (1,500 + 600 × a), where a is the acceleration in g. Setting that equal to 3,000 × a gives a = 1,500 ÷ 2,400 = 0.625 g. The rear tyres then carry 1,500 + 600 × 0.625 = 1,875 lb, and the traction limit is 1,875 lb of force. The 60-foot time at 0.625 g is √(120 ÷ (0.625 × 32.17)) = 2.44 s.

Why spinning loses time

Friction has two values. Static friction, where the contact patch grips and does not slide, is higher than kinetic friction, where it slips. A spinning tyre is sliding against the road, so it falls back to the lower figure. Suppose the kinetic coefficient here is 0.8 instead of 1.0, which is an assumption for the example. The same working gives a = (0.5 × 0.8) ÷ (1 − 0.8 × 0.2) = 0.476 g. The force at the road falls from 1,875 lb to about 1,430 lb, and the 60-foot time rises to 2.80 s.

That is 0.36 s lost at the very start, from the same car and the same engine. Power past the traction limit goes into heat and smoke, so the aim is to stay just under it.

Turning engine torque into force at the tyres

The force the engine asks of the tyres in first gear is torque × overall ratio ÷ tyre radius (ignoring drivetrain loss). Use the example four-speed from the gear ratio post: first gear 2.80 with a 3.42 final drive makes an overall ratio of 9.58. With 26-inch tyres the radius is 13 inches, or 1.083 ft. The engine reaches the 1,875 lb limit at 1,875 × 1.083 ÷ 9.58 = 212 lb-ft.

Between the two failures

The curve gives three regions. Above about 3,240 rpm the engine asks for more than the tyres can give, so a launch from there spins them. That is the wheelspin zone, and it covers most of the rev range.

Below it, the problem changes. When a car launches, the clutch or converter loads the engine suddenly and the revs sag. If the launch starts too low, the engine drops into a part of its curve with little torque and takes time to recover. That is bogging. In this example we will say the engine stumbles below about 2,500 rpm, where torque is 175 lb-ft, interpolating between 150 at 2,000 and 200 at 3,000.

The sweet spot is the window between, from 2,500 to 3,240 rpm here. It is nowhere near the 5,000 rpm torque peak: grip sets the launch RPM, not where the engine is strongest.

Launch RPM is about matching engine force to tyre grip, not about the biggest number. More grip moves the window up; less moves it down.

Reading it on the slip

The 60-foot time on a slip is the cleanest measure of a launch, because the first sixty feet are almost entirely a traction problem. The time slip post explains the lines. A spinning launch shows up as a slow 60-foot time even when the trap speed looks fine, as the 2.80 s against 2.44 s above shows.

Change one thing at a time, and repeat. Raise the launch RPM a little, run again, and watch the 60-foot line. If it falls, you were in the bog side. If it rises, you have crossed into wheelspin. The best value is the one just before the time turns the wrong way.
