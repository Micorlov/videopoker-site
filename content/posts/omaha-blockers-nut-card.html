A blocker is a card in your hand that an opponent therefore cannot hold. If you have the A♥, nobody else has it, so nobody else has the nut flush in hearts. The idea is simple. What is less obvious is how much it changes, and that the answer depends on how many cards you hold, which in Pot-Limit Omaha (PLO) is four. This post counts the opponent hands your cards remove, so that a blocker becomes a number rather than a feeling.

The setup

The flop is 9♥ 5♥ 2♥. You hold four cards, so 52 − 3 − 4 = 45 cards are unseen, and one opponent holds some four of them: C(45,4) = 148,995 possible hands. Of the 13 hearts, 3 are on the board, so 10 are not on the board, and some of those may be in your hand.

Because of the exactly-two rule, an opponent has a flush here only with at least two hearts in their hand. One heart plus the board is not enough. So count the four-card hands containing two or more hearts, for each number of hearts you hold yourself.

Every heart you hold takes a slice

If you hold h hearts, then 10 − h hearts are unseen. The number of opponent hands with at least two of them comes straight from the combinations:

The effect is large but gradual. Two hearts in your hand cut the opponent's flush hands from 20.9% to 14.0%, about a third fewer. Even holding four hearts leaves 8.0%, because six unseen hearts still pair up in 15 ways and fit with other cards. For comparison, a Hold'em opponent has only two cards: with eight hearts unseen the same count is C(8,2) ÷ C(47,2) = 28 ÷ 1,081 = 2.6%. Four-card hands put flushes in range far more often, and it is why a blocker matters more here.

The nut card is different

The table counts every flush. The ace changes which flush. Suppose you hold the K♥ and no other heart. The A♥ is unseen, and the opponent hands that hold it with at least one more heart (among the 8 other unseen hearts) number 6,104, which is 4.1% of all hands. Those are exactly the hands that make the nut flush.

Now suppose you hold the A♥ instead. Those 6,104 hands vanish, because the ace is in your hand. The opponent can still make a flush from 25,830 hands, but every one of them is a lower flush than any you could make with the ace. That is the whole value of the nut blocker: it does not cut the number of flushes much (both cases have 9 hearts unseen), it removes the top of the range.

What the nut flush share looks like when you have the flush

Hold two hearts without the ace and the opponent has 20,790 flush hands, of which 5,474 contain the ace (26.3%). That is the 3.7% single-opponent figure worked out in the second-best flush post, seen from the other side: 5,474 ÷ 148,995 = 3.7%. About one flush in four that your opponent could hold beats yours. Hold the A♥ with a second heart and that share is zero.

What a blocker does not do

  • It does not make your hand better. A♥ with three unrelated cards has no flush on this flop. It only means one specific opponent holding is impossible.
  • It does not lower the other threats. A full house or four of a kind needs a paired board. See paired boards and the nut flush for when the flush stops being the top hand.
  • It cuts hands, not certainty. After holding the ace, the opponent still has a flush 17.3% of the time with your one heart, and 14.0% with two.

Using it when you bluff

The same count works as a bluffing tool. An opponent who calls a big bet on a three-heart board is representing a flush. If you hold the A♥, you know they cannot have the nut flush, so the hands that call are lower flushes and sets or straights. If you hold the K♥ with nothing else, they can have the nuts in 4.1% of all hands, a gap you must set against how they have played. The blocker is a reason to bluff, never a guarantee, because it tells you what they cannot hold and nothing about what they do.

Practical rules

  1. Count unseen cards of the suit. Ten minus the hearts you hold, on a three-heart board. Then C(unseen, 2) is the flush pairs.
  2. Prefer holding the top card of a suit. It cuts the nuts out of an opponent's range, which a middle card cannot do.
  3. Remember the second heart. The ace without a second heart blocks the nuts but gives you no made flush.
  4. Use blockers to choose between close decisions, not to justify weak hands.
