Every blackjack chart has one pair that says the same thing against every dealer card: stand. Ten and ten is a 20, and 20 is beaten only by a dealer 21. Some players split it against a weak dealer card, thinking two bets in play must beat one. The sums say otherwise. Aces and eights are split to escape a poor total; tens are kept because the total is already excellent.

The rule

The chart in Blackjack Coach: Learn 21 is for six decks, dealer standing on soft 17, double after split and late surrender. For 10,10 it reads: stand against every dealer up-card. Any two ten-value cards, such as king and jack, are treated the same way.

What a 20 is worth

I worked out the dealer's final total from each up-card, drawing from an infinite deck with the dealer standing on all 17s. Ranks are equally likely except that the ten-value cards (10, J, Q, K) together make up 4 of 13. A 20 wins when the dealer busts or finishes on 17, 18 or 19, ties on 20 and loses to 21. Against a 6:

  • Dealer busts 42.32%, makes 17 16.54%, 18 10.63%, 19 10.63%.
  • Win: 42.32 + 16.54 + 10.63 + 10.63 = 80.11% (rounded from the unrounded figures).
  • Push, dealer 20: 10.17%.
  • Lose, dealer 21: 9.72%.

Per one-unit bet, that is 0.8011 − 0.0972 = +0.7040, so if you play a 20 against a 6 a hundred times you are ahead by about 70 bets on average. Even against the strongest low card, a dealer 2, the 20 wins 75.80%, pushes 12.40% and loses 11.80%.

What a split starts with

Split the tens and you place a second bet. Each ten becomes the first card of its own hand, and its second card comes from the deck:

  • A ten-value card, 4 in 13 = 30.77%: you are back to 20.
  • An ace, 1 in 13 = 7.69%: you hold 21. After a split this counts as 21, not as a blackjack, so it pays 1:1.
  • Anything else, 8 in 13 = 61.54%: a total of 12 to 19 that must be played on, with all the risk that brings.

So 38.46% of the time a new hand is as good as the 20 you had or better, and 61.54% of the time it is worse, often a stiff 12 to 16 that can bust. You also have two bets at risk instead of one.

Standing is ahead in every row. The split is closest against a 5 and a 6, which are the dealer's weakest cards, because those are the two up-cards that bust most often (41.64% and 42.32%). Even there the split gives up 0.13 to 0.16 of a bet. Against a 9, a pair of split tens is hurt by a dealer who finishes on 19 35.08% of the time, and the gap is more than half a bet.

Splitting can only win if the two new hands are worth more than the 20 you hold. Against a 6 each half would have to be worth +0.3520 on average, and they come to +0.2878.

Where this sits among the pairs

Against a 6 a 20 loses on fewer than one hand in ten. Pairs such as 8,8, which make a 16, start from the opposite position, as the aces and eights post explains. The 9,9 case is subtler, since 18 is also worth standing on against a 7 (see splitting nines against a 7).

I worked the split only for dealer cards 4 to 9. The dealer 10 and ace rows involve the peek for blackjack, so I leave them out; the chart stands there too.

The counting exception

There is one place where basic strategy is overridden: card counting. In Hi-Lo, the cards 2 to 6 count +1, 7 to 9 count 0, and tens and aces count −1. A high true count means the remaining shoe is rich in tens, so each split ten is more likely to draw another. The well-known Hi-Lo deviations (part of the Illustrious 18 list) say to split 10,10 against a 5 at a true count of +5 or higher, and against a 6 at +4 or higher. Below those counts, you stand. If you are not counting, the rule is simply never.

The running and true count are explained in Hi-Lo running and true count.

Remembering it

  1. 10,10 is a 20: stand against every dealer card.
  2. Against a 6, a 20 wins 80.11%, pushes 10.17% and loses 9.72%, worth +0.7040.
  3. A split starts two hands that are a 20 or a 21 only 38.46% of the time.
  4. The split is closest against a 5 or 6, and still trails by 0.13 to 0.16 of a bet.
  5. Only a Hi-Lo true count of +5 (against a 5) or +4 (against a 6) turns that around.
