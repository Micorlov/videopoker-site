A pair of nines is the pair where basic strategy changes its mind most often. It splits against a 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 and 9, and it stands against a 7, a 10 and an ace. The odd one out is the 7, which sits between two splitting cards. The explanation is in what the dealer ends up with, and it comes down to one fact: with a 7 showing, the dealer's most common final total is 17.

The rule in one line

The chart in Blackjack Coach: Learn 21 is for six decks, dealer standing on soft 17, double after split and late surrender. For 9,9 it reads:

  • Split against a dealer 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 or 9.
  • Stand against a 7, 10 or ace.

The two groups stand for different reasons. Against a 10 or an ace, 18 is not far enough ahead of a strong dealer to be worth breaking up. Against a 7, the reverse is true: 18 is very good, and nothing a split can offer improves on it.

What a dealer 7 finishes on

The dealer must draw to 17 and stand there. From a 7, the hole card alone gives 17 when it is a 10-value card, 4 ranks in 13. The full distribution, counting every rank as equally likely with 10, J, Q and K separate, and drawing from an infinite deck, comes out like this (my own recursion, with the dealer standing on all 17s):

The total is 36.86 + 26.23 + 13.78 + 7.86 + 7.86 + 7.41 = 100.00. The 17 comes from more routes than any other total. Besides the 10 in the hole, a 17 can be reached from a small hole card plus draws, for instance 7 and 3 and 7, or 7 and 2 and 8.

Where 18 stands

Your 18 beats a 17 and a bust, ties an 18, and loses to 19, 20 and 21:

  • Win: 36.86% + 26.23% = 63.09%
  • Push: 13.78%
  • Lose: 7.86% + 7.86% + 7.41% = 23.13%

Per one-unit bet that is 0.6309 − 0.2313 = +0.3996. Stand on 18 against a 7 a hundred times and you are ahead by about 40 bets on average. No dealer 7 can have a blackjack, so there is no peek complication in this working. Drawing instead is no help: only an ace, 2 or 3 improves 18 without busting, 3 ranks in 13.

Why the split loses here

Splitting turns one bet into two, so the comparison is +0.3996 against the total of both hands. I worked the split out too, again for an infinite deck, with these assumptions: a second bet on each nine, no resplitting, doubling allowed after the split, and each new hand played by the best of stand, hit or double. Only the 7 row favours standing. Against a 6 the dealer busts 42.32% of the time, so two nines each get a free run at a bust. Against an 8 the dealer lands on 18 in 35.93% of hands, so an 18 mostly pushes while a hand starting on 9 can still make 19 or 20. Against a 9 the standing 18 is a loser, and the split loses less. The 7 is different because it is the dealer card where your 18 is strongest, relative to what splitting can offer.

The gap is not wide. On these assumptions it is 0.0296 of a bet, small enough that the exact rules matter to the third decimal place, but the published charts all stand.

Look at the dealer's likely finish, not at your own total. Against a 7, the dealer ends on 17 more often than anything else, and your 18 beats it.

The ten and the ace

Against a 10 or an ace the dealer is strong, and the mechanism reverses. Standing on 18 is still the call, but for a different reason: a dealer 10 finishes on 20 more often than any other total, and splitting your nines would start two hands at 9 against a card that is hard to beat. I have not worked the split and stand values for these two rows, so I will not quote them. The chart stands.

Remembering it

  1. Dealer 7: stand. Their commonest total is 17, and 18 beats it.
  2. Dealer 10 or ace: stand. The dealer is too strong for a split to pay.
  3. Dealer 2 to 6, 8 or 9: split.
  4. Against a 7, standing on 18 wins 63.09%, pushes 13.78% and loses 23.13%.

The pair of eights follows a different rule, since it splits against everything (why you always split aces and eights). For how soft hands such as ace-seven treat the same dealer card, see the soft 18 post, and for the full bust-rate list, dealer bust rates.
