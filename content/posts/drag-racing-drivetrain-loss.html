A car advertised at 400 horsepower does not put 400 horsepower on the road. The figure on the spec sheet is taken at the crankshaft. By the time the power has passed through the clutch or torque converter, the gearbox, the driveshaft, the differential and the axles, some of it has gone. What reaches the tyres is lower, and it is the lower figure that moves the car.

Two dynos, two numbers

An engine dyno bolts to the crankshaft and measures what the engine makes on its own. A chassis dyno, also called a rolling road, holds the car with its driven wheels on rollers and measures what arrives there. Same engine, same pull, two readings. The difference is drivetrain loss.

The loss is mechanical work. Gears mesh and bearings turn, and each does a little friction work. Oil is churned by spinning gears. In an automatic, the torque converter shears fluid, and that slip turns power into heat. Differentials and driveshafts add their own friction, and the wheels, tyres and rollers have rotating mass that has to be spun up before anything is left to measure. None of it is a fault. It is the cost of carrying power across a car.

The arithmetic, both ways

Going from crank to wheels, multiply by one minus the loss. With 400 crank horsepower and a 15% loss: 400 × 0.85 = 340 at the wheels.

Going the other way, divide. A chassis dyno reads 340 and you assume 15%: 340 ÷ 0.85 = 400. Do not add 15% to 340. That gives 391, because the 15% was taken from the larger number. Dividing by 0.85 is the same as adding about 17.6%, and the difference matters when you are comparing against someone else’s claim.

Why a flat percentage is only an approximation

A single percentage hides how the loss behaves. Much of it is parasitic: drag from oil, bearings and seals that depends on how fast things spin and hardly at all on how much power is going through. The rest scales with power. A better model is a mostly fixed amount plus a proportional part.

Take an invented example, not a measurement: a fixed 20 hp plus 5% of crank power. At 200 hp the loss is 20 + 10 = 30 hp, which is 15%. At 400 hp it is 20 + 20 = 40 hp, or 10%. At 800 hp it is 20 + 40 = 60 hp, or 7.5%. The loss in horsepower rises, but the percentage falls.

That is why quoting 15% for everything is wrong, and also why it is useful. It is a decent first estimate for an ordinary car, and it overstates the loss on a very powerful one. Treat the numbers above as typical, not constant. Gearbox type, oil temperature, tyre pressure and the dyno itself all shift the figure from one car to the next.

What it means on the strip

Elapsed time is set mainly by power against weight, and the power that counts is the power at the wheels. One empirical relation, usually credited to Fox, puts quarter-mile ET at roughly 5.825 times the cube root of weight in pounds divided by wheel horsepower. For a 3,400 lb car on 340 wheel horsepower the ratio is 10, its cube root is about 2.15, and the estimate is about 12.5 seconds. Hale’s version uses a larger constant, about 6.3, for crank power. Both are fitted to typical results, not laws of physics, and both can miss by a few tenths. The power-to-weight post sets out the idea in more detail.

It is not a straight loss, though. A heavier automatic or all-wheel-drive car gives up power at the wheels, but it may take that power back at the start. All-wheel drive puts the driven load on four tyres instead of two, and an automatic can hold the engine in its power band through the launch without a clutch drop. Off the line, traction limits how much power can be used, so a car with less wheel power that grips can beat one with more that spins. That also depends on weight moving onto the rear tyres, which the weight transfer post explains. The trade is real, and which side wins depends on the car, the surface and the tyres.

Compare like with like

Two practical rules follow. First, a crank figure and a wheel figure are not the same measurement. A claim of 400 and a reading of 340 are consistent for the same car, not a contradiction. Second, only compare dyno numbers taken on the same kind of dyno. Chassis dynos differ in how they hold the car, how they correct for weather and how they account for their own losses, so two readings from different machines can disagree by several percent for the same car. If you are testing a change, such as a different tyre or a new tune, use the same dyno, the same day if you can, and look at the change rather than the absolute number.
