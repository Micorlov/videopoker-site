A pick bonus asks you to tap hidden objects and collect what is behind them. It feels as if the choice matters. In most slots it does not, and the reason is simple enough to check with a pencil. This post uses invented numbers throughout. They are an illustration of how such rounds are commonly built, not figures from any particular game.

Two ways to build a pick round

Design A: decide first, reveal later. When the bonus triggers, the game's random number generator (RNG) draws the outcome from a table. Only then does the picking screen appear. Each tap shows the next piece of a result that already exists. Tapping the left tile or the right tile changes what you see first, not what you are paid.

Design B: a shuffled board. The game lays out a set of prizes in random positions, and your taps choose positions. Here the picks do select the prize, but you cannot see behind a tile, so every unopened tile is equally likely to hold any remaining prize. A choice made with no information cannot beat a random one.

Either way, the player has no lever to pull. The difference is only whether the randomness is spent before the picking or during it.

Design A with numbers

Suppose a bonus pays a total of 5, 10, 20 or 50 times the bet, drawn when the bonus opens with these weights. The table is made up.

The expected value is the sum of each prize times its chance: 2.5 + 3.0 + 3.0 + 2.5 = 11 times the bet. That is the average bonus over a very large number of bonus rounds. It says nothing about the next one: half of all bonuses pay only 5 times the bet, and one in twenty pays 50.

Now the picks. Suppose the draw gave 20 times the bet and the screen has three tiles to open. Whether you tap them in the order 1-2-3 or 3-1-2, the total is 20 times the bet. The game may split it into three displayed prizes, but the split was settled with the total. This is why developers can program the animation, the near-miss tension and the sound without any of it touching the outcome.

If the result is drawn when the bonus triggers, the picks are theatre: they change the order of the reveal and nothing else.

Design B with numbers

Take a board of nine hidden tiles holding these prizes, in multiples of the bet: 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 5, 10 and 25. They add up to 50, so the average tile is 50 ÷ 9 = 5.56. You open three tiles and keep what is behind them.

There are 9 × 8 × 7 ÷ (3 × 2 × 1) = 84 different sets of three tiles. Because the board was shuffled out of sight, each set is equally likely, so the expected total is three times the average tile: 3 × 5.56 = 16.67 times the bet. Averaging all 84 sets one by one gives the same figure.

  • Chance of finding the 25. It is behind 28 of the 84 sets, which is 28 ÷ 84 = 1 in 3, or simply 3 tiles out of 9.
  • Range. The worst set is 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 times the bet and the best is 25 + 10 + 5 = 40.
  • Strategy. Picking corners, the centre or the tiles that glow gives the same 16.67. Nothing in the layout points to the 25, so every rule for choosing is equivalent to guessing.

Choice only matters when the board offers information, for example when some prizes are shown before you decide. Even then the information has to be real, not a coloured hint that the animation draws regardless.

Telling the designs apart

From the outside you often cannot see which design you are playing. The useful habit is to ask what information the screen actually gives you before you pick. If it gives none, the choice cannot be better than a guess, and the honest summary is that the picks are not a skill.

The same reasoning is behind why a slot is never due: a random draw has no memory, and nothing you do on the screen alters the draw. How often a bonus opens, and how spread out its prizes are, belongs to the numbers described in slot RTP and volatility.
