Fifty-two cards, five at a time, order ignored: that is 52 × 51 × 50 × 49 × 48 divided by 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1, which comes to 2,598,960 possible hands. Every one is equally likely on a fair shuffle, so the chance of any category is just the number of hands in it divided by that total. No simulation is needed. Each count below is exact, and each one can be checked with a pencil.

Where each count comes from

Every row is a short piece of counting. Choose the ranks, then choose the suits.

  1. Straight flush (40, including the 4 royals). A straight can start on any of ten ranks, ace-low (A-2-3-4-5) up to ace-high (10-J-Q-K-A), in any of four suits: 10 × 4 = 40.
  2. Four of a kind (624). Thirteen ranks for the four, and 48 cards left for the fifth: 13 × 48.
  3. Full house (3,744). Thirteen ranks for the trips with 4 ways to pick three suits, then 12 ranks for the pair with 6 ways to pick two suits: 13 × 4 × 12 × 6.
  4. Flush (5,108). There are 1,287 ways to pick five different ranks from thirteen. Ten of those are straights, leaving 1,277, in each of four suits: 1,277 × 4.
  5. Straight (10,200). Ten rank patterns, and each of the five cards can be any of four suits, giving 1,024 suit patterns. Take out the 4 where all five suits match, because those are straight flushes: 10 × 1,020.
  6. Three of a kind (54,912). Thirteen ranks for the trips, 4 suit choices, then two different other ranks from the remaining twelve (66 ways), each in any suit (16 ways): 13 × 4 × 66 × 16.
  7. Two pair (123,552). Two ranks for the pairs (78 ways), 6 suit choices for each pair, and 44 cards for the fifth that match neither: 78 × 36 × 44.
  8. One pair (1,098,240). Thirteen ranks, 6 suit choices, then three different ranks from twelve (220 ways), each in any of 4 suits: 13 × 6 × 220 × 64.
  9. High card (1,302,540). The same 1,277 non-straight rank patterns as the flush, each with the 1,020 suit patterns that are not all one suit: 1,277 × 1,020.

Five different ranks is the shape behind four rows: straight flush, flush, straight and high card. Only sequence and matching suits separate them.

Three things the table says

A flush is about half as common as a straight. There are 5,108 flushes and 10,200 straights, a ratio of very nearly two to one. A straight only needs its ranks to line up, and any of 1,020 suit patterns will do. A flush needs all five suits to match, which is only 4 of the 1,024 possible suit patterns.

Two pair is more than twice as common as three of a kind. The ratio is 2.25 to one. Three matching cards are harder to collect than two separate pairs, so trips rank higher.

Half of all hands are no pair at all. High card is 50.12% of deals, and one pair is another 42.26%. Together they make up 92.4% of everything you will ever be dealt. Two pair or better arrives on about 7.6% of hands, and anything from a straight upwards on about 0.76%: roughly one deal in 132.

Ace-high alone is nearly one deal in five, more common than every hand from two pair upwards put together. At the other end, seven-high is the worst hand there is, and it comes in only four rank patterns: 7-6-5-4-2, 7-6-5-3-2, 7-6-4-3-2 and 7-5-4-3-2. Nothing lower is possible. The only five different ranks no higher than six are 2-3-4-5-6, which is a straight, and adding an ace makes the ace the high card.

Categories versus hand values

Once ranks inside each category count (a pair of eights against a pair of nines, or which kickers sit beside them), the 2,598,960 hands collapse into 7,462 distinct values. Suits never break a tie in standard rules, which is why that number is so much smaller. Each value belongs to exactly one row of the first table, so a hand from a higher row beats any hand from a lower one.

All of this is the maths of five cards dealt once from a full deck. Exchanging cards or building a hand one card at a time changes the chances of finishing with each hand, but not these base counts.
