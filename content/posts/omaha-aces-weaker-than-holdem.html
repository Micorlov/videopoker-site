Aces are the best pair in any poker game that uses a standard deck. In Hold'em, though, they are nearly a lock against one opponent, and in Omaha they are only a good favourite. The cards are the same. What changes is how many hands the opponent can make with the board.

All equity figures below are approximate. They come from widely published simulations and shift by a point or two depending on the source and, in Omaha, on the side cards and suits.

The numbers side by side

Pocket aces in Hold'em win about 85% of the time against one random hand, and about 82% against the next best start, pocket kings. Add opponents and the figure falls quickly: about 73% against two random hands and about 64% against three.

In Omaha, a hand with two aces and two side cards wins roughly 65-70% against one random four-card hand. The best versions, such as A-A-K-K with two suits each, sit at the top of that range. A pair of aces with two unconnected, unsuited cards sits lower. So heads-up in Omaha, aces are about as strong as Hold'em aces against three opponents.

Reason 1: opponents hold pairs far more often

A random Hold'em hand is a pair 78 times in 1,326, or 5.9%. A random Omaha hand has four cards, and the chance they are all different ranks is

C(13,4) × 44 ÷ C(52,4) = 715 × 256 ÷ 270,725 = 183,040 ÷ 270,725 = 67.6%

so a random Omaha hand contains at least one pair 32.4% of the time. Since only two of the four cards play, the opponent usually picks their best two, but a pair, a pair of draws, or two ways to connect are all on offer at once.

Reason 2: more hands to beat your pair

In Hold'em the opponent has one two-card hand to improve. In Omaha the opponent can combine any two of four hole cards with any three of five board cards, which is 6 × 10 = 60 hands at the river, against 21 in Hold'em (see the exactly-two rule). More ways to make a hand means a better chance that one of them beats a single pair.

Your aces gain nothing from this, because they are still a single pair on most boards. The chance of flopping an ace for a set is only slightly better than in Hold'em. In Hold'em, with 50 cards unseen and two aces left, it is 1 − (48 × 47 × 46) ÷ (50 × 49 × 48) = 288 ÷ 2,450 = 11.8%. In Omaha, with 48 unseen and the same two aces left, it is 1 − (46 × 45 × 44) ÷ (48 × 47 × 46) = 276 ÷ 2,256 = 12.2%. Neither is common. Most of the time your aces are an overpair on a flop that opponents can hit with draws.

Reason 3: draws catch up

An overpair is the kind of hand that draws catch. In Hold'em the biggest straight draw is eight outs; in Omaha a hand can have up to 20 (see wraps). Flush draws are also easier to come by, since four cards give more chances of holding two of one suit than two cards do. Against an overpair, an opponent with a big draw is often close to level, and sometimes ahead.

That is why Omaha equity is so compressed. Hold'em has a big spread, with aces at 85% and a weak hand like 7-2 at around 35% against a random hand. Omaha hands cluster much closer together, and even the top starts are rarely above 70%.

What to do with that

  1. Look at the side cards. A-A-K-K and A-A-J-T with suits have an ace pair plus a way to connect or make a flush. A-A-7-2 of mixed suits has only the pair, and it sits at the low end of the range or below it.
  2. Aces want a nut-flush draw. An ace with a suited side card gives the nut flush draw for free, which is a hand that can win without improving to a set.
  3. Do not stack off on an overpair. After the flop, pair-only hands are the ones that lose to wraps and draws. A flop of J-T-9 or three of one suit makes aces weak. See second-best flush for the same logic with flushes.

In Hold'em, aces are a hand to build the pot with. In Omaha they are a good start to be careful with: a favourite, but nowhere near the 85% that Hold'em teaches you to expect.

A quick check

You can test the idea without any maths. Deal yourself A-A-K-K and one random four-card hand a few dozen times, and count how often the aces hold up. Expect them to win around two times in three, not five in six.
