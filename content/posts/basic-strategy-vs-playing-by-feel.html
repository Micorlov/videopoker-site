Basic strategy is the set of plays that gives up the least in the long run against a fixed set of rules. “Better than guessing” is not much use as a statement, so this post puts a size on it and splits that size into the mistakes that cause it. Some figures are calculations and some are commonly quoted estimates; I mark which is which.

Three ways to play the same game

The house edge is the average share of your initial bets that the game keeps over the long run. For a six-deck game with ordinary rules, perfect basic strategy gives the house roughly half a percent. That figure is established: it comes from working out the value of every play against every dealer card, and published tables agree to within a tenth of a point or so, depending on the exact rules.

The 2% figure is an estimate of what a typical improviser gives away. It is not a measurement of you: someone who gets two or three plays wrong costs far less. Read it as “a couple of points” and no more precise.

“Never bust” is a precise strategy, so it can be calculated: stand on every hard total from 12 up, hit soft 17 and below, and never double or split. On an infinite deck, which is close enough to six decks for this purpose, I get about 6 points. Other versions of the idea land elsewhere, so read it as “several points”. It sounds cautious and is the most expensive style of the three.

Where the gap comes from

Take the same infinite-deck calculation and switch off one skill at a time, leaving basic strategy in place everywhere else. Each result is the extra house edge, in points, that the leak adds. These are my own approximations and the true six-deck values will differ by a few hundredths, but the order does not change.

  1. Standing on 12 to 16 against a 7 or higher: about 3.4 points. This is the “don’t risk busting” instinct, and it is the single most expensive habit. Against a strong dealer card your stiff total loses more often by standing than by drawing.
  2. Hitting 12 to 16 against a 2 to 6: about 2.5 points. The mirror image. Those dealer cards bust often, so the chart stands on 13 to 16 and lets the dealer do the damage. Hitting there is a lost chance, not a safe play.
  3. Never doubling: about 1.5 points. Doubling is the main way a good start earns more than one bet. Doubling 9, 10 and 11 covers the hard totals that matter most.
  4. Never splitting: about 0.5 points. Smaller overall because pairs are rare.
  5. Always taking insurance: about 0.3 points. The dealer shows an ace about one hand in 13, and the side bet is half a stake that returns about 7.4% less than it takes. That is 0.5 × 7.4% ÷ 13, close to 0.3 of a point. This one is arithmetic you can check yourself, and the insurance post shows the working.

The first two together are about 5.9 points, which is more than the whole typical-player estimate. That is no contradiction, since nobody makes every error at every opportunity. It does show that the two stiff-hand rules are where the money is.

The leak that costs most per occurrence

The ranking above is by cost across all your hands, which mixes how often a spot comes up with how much it costs when it does. Per occurrence the order changes completely. Using the same calculation, here is what a single mistake costs, as a share of the initial bet.

  • Not splitting a pair of aces against a 6: about 0.5 of a bet. Two hands starting from an ace are worth about 0.67 of a bet; the single soft 12 is worth about 0.19.
  • Not splitting eights against a 6: about 0.5 of a bet. Splitting turns a hard 16, which loses about 0.15 of a bet even when played well, into two hands worth about +0.33 in total.
  • Not doubling 11 against a 6: about 0.3 of a bet.
  • Misplaying a stiff, such as hitting 14 against a 6 or standing on it against a 7: about 0.15.
  • Taking insurance: about 0.04.

So the costliest single mistake is failing to split aces or eights, and the cheapest is insurance. Pairs rank lower in the first list only because a given pair arrives about once in 169 hands, so a pair of aces or eights turns up on roughly one hand in 85 between them. A stiff 12 to 16 turns up far more often than that, and even a cost of 0.15 per hand, repeated that often, outweighs a rare 0.5.

Frequency times cost is what ranks a leak. Splitting aces and eights is the dearest play to miss and still ranks fourth, because it happens rarely.

What to fix first

The order follows from the numbers. Learn the stiff hands first: when to stand on 12 to 16 against a 2 to 6, and when to draw against a 7 or higher. That is two short rules and it removes the two biggest leaks. Next learn the doubles, then the pairs, and treat insurance as the easy one, since the rule is simply to decline it. The learning-order post sets out the full sequence, so I will not repeat it here.

One caveat on every figure above: they use an infinite deck without surrender or resplitting, so the six-deck game differs a little. The shape does not shift. Frequent decisions cost most overall; rare ones cost most each time.
