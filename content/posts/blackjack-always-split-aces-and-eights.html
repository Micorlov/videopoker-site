Most of basic strategy depends on the dealer's up-card. Aces and eights are the exception. Against a 2, against a 10, against an ace, the advice is the same: put a second bet beside the first and play two hands. It feels wrong with eights in particular, because a pair of eights looks like a decent hand. The numbers show why it is not.

Sixteen is the worst total

Two eights add up to 16, and 16 is the hand every blackjack player dreads. The reason is that both ways of playing it go badly.

Standing on 16 relies on the dealer busting. Against a ten-value up-card the dealer busts 21.2% of the time (see how the dealer plays and how often each up-card busts). A dealer who does not bust finishes on 17 or more, and 16 beats none of those. So you win 21.2% of hands and lose the other 78.8%, a net loss of 0.788 − 0.212 = 0.576 of your bet on every hand.

Hitting 16 has a different problem. Treating every rank as equally likely, a 6, 7, 8, 9 or any of the four ten-value ranks takes you over 21. That is 8 ranks in 13, or 61.5%. More than half the time you bust before the dealer has to do anything.

Splitting is the way out. You are not asked to play 16. You are asked to play two hands that each start with a single 8.

Why one 8 is so much better than two

An 8 on its own cannot bust with the next card, because no card takes it past 19. Of the 13 ranks, a 9 makes 17, any ten-value card makes 18 and an ace makes a soft 19. That is 1 + 4 + 1 = 6 ranks in 13, or 46.2%, giving you a decent hand straight away with no risk of going over.

The cost is a second bet of the same size, and against a strong up-card the two hands still lose more often than they win. Even then, basic strategy shows the split loses less than standing or hitting the combined 16.

Aces work for the opposite reason

A pair of aces is awkward to count. Played as one hand it is 12, because only one ace can count as 11 (two would make 22). Twelve is a poor total with a mild bust risk: only a ten-value card spoils it, 4 ranks in 13, or 30.8%.

Split them and each ace is a hand of 11, or in effect the strongest starting card in the pack. The next card can only improve it. A ten-value card makes 21, which happens 4 times in 13, or 30.8%, per hand. Any 2 to 9 gives a soft total from 13 to 20 that you can often keep improving, and a second ace gives another pair, which some tables let you split again.

Both rules are one idea: the pair is worth less together than apart.

Where the other pairs fit

The chart makes the logic of the whole pairs table visible. Tens are a strong total, so you stand on them: a pair of tens is 20, the second-best hand you can have, and splitting it swaps a near-certain winner for two hands that start on 10. Nines are 18, which basic strategy splits against most dealer cards but stands on against a 7, a 10 or an ace; a dealer 7 often finishes on 17, and 18 already beats that.

Two fives are a 10, a hand to double or hit, not to break up. Fours, threes and twos, and sevens and sixes, are the cases where the dealer's card decides. Split them when it is weak, roughly a 2 to 6 or 7 (fours only against a 5 or 6, and only where doubling after a split is allowed), because the dealer is then the one likely to bust. The exact ranges vary a little with the table's rules. Only aces and eights skip that question.

Checking the claims yourself

Every figure here comes from counting ranks out of 13, which makes it a good habit to keep. For a hard total, count the ranks that would take it past 21: 16 loses to 6 through 10-value (8 ranks), 12 only to the tens (4 ranks). For a single starting 8, nothing busts. For an ace, only the four ten-value ranks reach 21 in one card. Cards already on the table shift these slightly, but not the ordering or the advice.
