A pool table is a flat piece of slate with cloth stretched over it, and the cloth decides how the balls behave once they are moving. Two tables with identical dimensions, like the 9-foot 100 × 50 in surface in the table sizes post, can need very different shot speeds.

Worsted and woollen cloth

Both types are wool, but they are made differently. Worsted cloth is woven from long wool fibres that have been combed straight and spun tightly, then woven and shaved smooth. The result is hard, thin and napless: the surface has no fibres lying in a preferred direction. Most pool cloth sold for tournament tables is worsted or a worsted wool and nylon blend, and it is the fast option.

Woollen baize is made from shorter fibres that are carded rather than combed, so they point in many directions. The cloth is brushed after weaving to raise a soft pile. That pile lies one way, which is the nap. It is thicker, softer and slower than worsted, and it is what English billiards and snooker tables have traditionally used, with the nap running from the baulk end towards the top cushion.

Why nap changes the roll

A rolling ball sinks a little into the cloth and keeps climbing out of the dent it makes. That is rolling resistance, and it is what slows a ball that is not sliding. Fibres leaning in the direction of travel offer less resistance than fibres leaning against it, so a ball rolled with the nap runs slightly further and one rolled against it stops slightly sooner.

A slow ball rolled across the nap can also drift the way the fibres lie. Snooker players learn this as part of weight and line. On napless pool cloth it is small enough to ignore, which is one reason the games in the pool and snooker post feel so different.

Rolling resistance sets the distance

The distance a ball rolls follows a simple rule. A rolling ball slows at a steady rate a = μ × g, where μ is the cloth's coefficient of rolling resistance and g is 9.81 m/s². The distance is then d = v² / (2 × μ × g). Published measurements put μ for pool cloth at roughly 0.01, though it varies from cloth to cloth. Take 0.01 as a working number, not a specification:

  • Deceleration is 0.01 × 9.81, about 0.098 m/s².
  • A ball rolling at 1 m/s therefore travels 1 / (2 × 0.098), about 5.1 m, before it stops.
  • The 100 in length of a 9-foot table is 2.54 m. To just reach the far cushion needs v = √(2 × 0.098 × 2.54), about 0.7 m/s.

The two relationships to remember hold whatever the exact μ. Distance is proportional to 1 / μ, so a cloth with half the resistance sends the same shot twice as far. And the speed needed for a given distance is proportional to √μ, so a cloth with double the resistance needs √2 = 1.41 times the speed, about 41% more, to travel the same distance.

So the same stroke that leaves the cue ball on the right spot on a slow table sends it long on a fast one. Pace does not transfer exactly between cloths, and the position play post assumes you have a working sense of yours.

What humidity does

Wool absorbs water vapour from the air. As the fibres take up moisture they swell, the pile fattens and stiffens, and it lies less evenly. A ball now sinks into a thicker, damper surface, so rolling resistance goes up. The results are a slower table and a ball that is easier to push off line, especially a slow one near the end of its run.

Cushions respond too. Cushion rubber is covered in the same cloth, and a damp cloth grips and absorbs more of the ball's speed, so the rebound is deader and a bank shot comes back shorter than you expect. Banks, covered in the bank shot post, then need extra pace.

Cold air holds less water vapour, so an unheated room is often a damp one. Expect its table to play slower than the same table in a warm, dry room.

Why snooker tables are heated

This is why professional snooker tables carry heating elements under the slate. The slate is kept at about 40 to 45 °C at its surface. That is well above room temperature, and it keeps the cloth dry and the pace consistent from one session to the next, whatever the weather outside. Heating removes humidity as a variable; it is not there to make the table fast.

Test the table before you play

A length-of-table roll is the quickest check:

  1. Place the cue ball behind the head string and hit it gently so it rolls the full 100 in to the foot cushion on a 9-foot table (88 in on an 8-footer).
  2. Note where it stops after the rebound. The further back it comes, the faster the table.
  3. Repeat in the other direction. On a napped table the two rolls can differ.

Then adjust: firmer on a slow, cold or damp table, softer on a fast one. The change is to speed, not aim, so cut angles carry over unchanged. The cue ball spin post covers how spin interacts with the cloth.
