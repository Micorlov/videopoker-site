A hard 12 is the hand most players stand on too early. It cannot be improved by much, it can be spoiled by one card, and the dealer is showing a small card. Against a 2 or a 3, basic strategy still says to hit. The reason is a comparison between two numbers, how often you bust and how often the dealer does, and the gap between them is bigger than it looks.

The rule

Hard 12 means a total of 12 with no ace counted as 11. Against a dealer 2 or 3 you hit. Against a 4, 5 or 6 you stand. Against a 7 or higher you hit, as with every stiff total from 12 to 16. Against a 2 to 6 you stand on 13 to 16, so 12 is the only stiff total that draws against a 2 or 3. What one card does to a hard 12

A hard 12 is the most forgiving of the stiff totals, because only one kind of card breaks it. Counting each of the 13 ranks as equally likely, with the four ten-value ranks (10, J, Q, K) counted separately, the next card does this:

The next card after hitting a hard 12, 13 ranks treated as equally likely.

So 30.8% of the time you lose on the spot, 38.5% of the time you land on 17 or better, and the remaining 30.8% leave you on a total that is still weak but no worse than the 12 you started with. In 52 cards that is 16 tens, and a real shoe shifts the figure only slightly.

Notice what the 13 to 16 outcomes are. Against a 2 or a 3 you stand on all of them.

What the dealer does

If you stand on 12, you win only if the dealer busts. Your whole chance of winning is the dealer's bust rate. These are the six-deck figures from the dealer bust-rate post, with the dealer standing on every 17:

Standing on 12 against a 2 wins 35.4% of the time and against a 3 wins 37.4%. Those look like decent numbers beside a 30.8% bust risk, and that is the trap. The 30.8% is only one outcome of the draw, and the draw also gives you a 38.5% chance of 17 or better, a hand that beats a dealer 2 or 3 far more often than a bare 12 does.

How close it is

This is a close decision, and the sensible way to say so is with numbers. I worked out the average result per unit bet with an infinite deck, the dealer standing on all 17s, and best play after any hit, which keeps the calculation exact and independent of the shoe. A six-deck shoe moves the figures slightly, so treat them as the size of the gap.

Every figure is negative. A hard 12 loses on average against every dealer card, so the choice is between a bigger and a smaller loss. Hitting against a 2 loses about 3.9 units per hundred less than standing, and against a 3 about 1.9 less. Against a 4 the two plays are almost level, which is why that cell is the one you will see disagree between charts that assume different rules or a different number of decks. Against a 5 or a 6 the dealer busts often enough that waiting beats drawing.

Why the line falls between 3 and 4

As the dealer's bust rate rises, standing gets better, because your only way to win by standing is for the dealer to fail. The cost of hitting stays fixed: 30.8% of the time you lose before the dealer plays. The dealer's 2 and 3 are the two weakest of the weak cards, busting about one hand in three, so a stand wins too seldom to beat the draw. Once the bust rate reaches about 40%, at the 4, the two plays meet, and beyond that standing is ahead.

Memory aid: stand on 12 only when the dealer shows 4, 5 or 6. Against a 2 or 3 the dealer is weak but not weak enough, and you should hit.
