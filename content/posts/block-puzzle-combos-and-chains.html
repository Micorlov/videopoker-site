The two words are often used loosely. In this post a combo is several lines clearing from a single placement, and a chain is lines clearing on consecutive placements, one after another. I do not know how the app counts either toward its goals, so I stick to what can be checked: cells and geometry on a 9x9 grid where rows, columns and 3x3 boxes all clear.

Counting the overlaps

One row, one column or one box is 9 cells. Two lines cleared together are not always 18, because cells they share are only cleared once. The rule is inclusion-exclusion: add the sizes, then take off the overlaps.

  • Two rows (or two columns, or two boxes) share nothing: 9 + 9 = 18.
  • A row and a column cross at exactly one cell: 9 + 9 − 1 = 17.
  • A row and a box that the row passes through share 3 cells: 9 + 9 − 3 = 15. A column and a box it passes through also share 3, so that is 15 as well.

If the row misses the box altogether there is no overlap and the answer is 18 again. The earlier post on the 9x9 grid has the wider overlap counts.

Three lines at once

Take a row, a column and a box. The answer depends on the arrangement, so here is the case I drew: the row and the column both pass through the box, which means they must cross inside it. The overlaps are then row and column 1, row and box 3, column and box 3, and the crossing cell is in all three lines.

Inclusion-exclusion adds the three sizes, subtracts the pair overlaps and adds back the cell that was subtracted too often: 9 + 9 + 9 − (1 + 3 + 3) + 1 = 27 − 7 + 1 = 21. A direct count agrees: the box's 9 cells, plus the 6 of the row outside the box, plus the 6 of the column outside it, is 9 + 6 + 6 = 21.

Move the column so that it misses the box, crossing the row outside it. Now the pair overlaps are 1, 3 and 0, and no cell is in all three: 27 − (1 + 3 + 0) + 0 = 23. The direct count is 9 for the box, 6 for the row outside it, and 8 for the column, which loses the one cell it shares with the row: 9 + 6 + 8 = 23.

More lines do not guarantee more cells. A row, column and box crossing inside the box (21) clear only 3 more than two rows (18), because the box is overlapped on two sides. Only the arrangement with the column outside the box reaches 23.

These are the shapes a combo has to fill. The Adventure levels in Big Ben Blocks: Block Puzzle set combo goals, so it is worth knowing which gaps one piece can reach before you start building towards them.

One cell short on two lines

A line that is one cell short has 8 of its 9 cells filled. Keep two lines in that state and you are holding two cells open, and what happens next depends on where they are.

  • The same cell. If a row and a column are both short at their crossing, the one gap serves both. Filling it clears 17 cells. Before the piece lands, 8 + 8 = 16 cells are filled, since the crossing is empty and the two lines share nothing else.
  • Two rows, gaps in the same column. A piece covering both stacked gaps clears 18 cells, with 16 of them filled beforehand.
  • Gaps that do not touch. No single piece covers both, so you need two placements. That is a chain, not a combo.

The buy is 17 or 18 cells from one placement. The cost is that the held-open cells must stay reachable. A piece that fills only one of the two gaps clears one line and leaves the other still short, and a gap that few pieces can reach is a liability the longer you hold it. The posts on setting up double clears and holes and islands look at both sides of that trade.

Why a chain needs more than a combo

A combo needs one thing: a single piece whose cells cover every gap. A chain needs that twice, in order, and the first clear changes the board the second depends on.

Clearing a row empties 9 cells. Each of the 9 columns loses one filled cell, and each of the 3 boxes the row passes through loses 3. So any line that crosses the cleared one ends up further from full than it was. Parallel lines are not affected, since two rows never share a cell. That is why a chain of rows, or of columns, is the easier kind to plan, while a row followed by a column has to refill the crossing cell that the first clear just emptied.

The second requirement is the pieces. Even with the geometry right, the next piece has to be one that fits the gap you left. In an Adventure level each level has a fixed starting board and piece sequence, so you can work out in advance whether the next piece will fit, and repeat a plan that works. The Adventure levels are where chain planning can be rehearsed rather than hoped for.

What the counts tell you

A combo is a property of the board at the moment of one placement: gaps that one piece can cover, and a cell count you can work out beforehand, 17 for a row and column, 18 for two rows, 21 or 23 for three lines. A chain is a property of a sequence: each clear must leave the next line one piece away. Build for a combo when the gaps line up, and for a chain when the lines run in parallel.
