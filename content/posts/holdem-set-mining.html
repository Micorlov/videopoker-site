Set mining means calling a raise with a small pocket pair for one reason: to flop three of a kind, a set, and win a large pot from a hand that cannot easily fold. The pair itself rarely wins unimproved, so the whole plan rests on one event. Here is how often it happens, what that does to the price you can pay, and why a set is not always safe.

Counting the flops that give you a set

You hold a pair, say two sevens. Fifty cards are unseen, and two of them are sevens. The flop is three of those fifty, so there are C(50,3) = 19,600 possible flops, all equally likely.

It is easier to count the flops that miss. A miss means none of the three flop cards is a seven, so all three come from the 48 other cards: C(48,3) = 17,296 flops. The rest give you a set or better:

19,600 − 17,296 = 2,304 flops, and 2,304 ÷ 19,600 = 11.8%.

The odds against are 17,296 to 2,304, which is 7.5 to 1. Of those 2,304 flops, 2,112 give a plain set, 144 give a full house because the board also holds a pair, and 48 give four of a kind (both remaining sevens plus any of 48 cards). The figure holds for every pair from deuces to aces, because only the two remaining cards of your rank matter.

Seeing more cards helps less than you hope

The same method works for later streets. With four board cards the miss count is C(48,4) out of C(50,4), so the chance of a set or better is 1 − 194,580 ÷ 230,300 = 15.5%. With all five it is 1 − C(48,5) ÷ C(50,5) = 1 − 1,712,304 ÷ 2,118,760 = 19.2%, or about 4.2 to 1 against.

That 19.2% is a ceiling, not a plan. To see the turn and river you usually have to call more bets, and a bet on a board that has not helped you is where a pair of sevens folds. Treat 11.8% as the number the call has to justify.

What the 7.5 to 1 does to the price

Call a raise and put in one unit. Most of the time you miss and the unit is gone. Set the expected result to zero: 0.1176 × W = 0.8824 × 1, so W = 7.5. You need to win about 7.5 times your call, on average, each time the set arrives. It is the break-even logic of the pot odds post, with a hit chance in place of a draw.

The pot holds only a few times your call, usually the blinds and the raise. The rest of the 7.5 has to come from your opponent's stack, and they will not always pay you off. That is the job of implied odds.

The familiar rule of thumb says the effective stack, the smaller of the two stacks, should be 15 to 20 times the amount you call. The factor of two over 7.5 allows for not being paid in full. Suppose that, when you hit, you collect on average half of what is behind. A stack of 15 times the call then returns 7.5 times the call, break-even before the pot's own contribution; at 20 times it returns 10. Calling 3 big blinds needs 45 to 60 behind; calling 10 needs 150 to 200. "Half of what is behind" is an assumption, not a law, so the rule shows whether a call is plausible. It does not prove it pays. Shorter stacks make the call worse, because even a paid-off set cannot then win 7.5 calls.

When a set is not the best hand

A set is strong, not unbeatable. The scenario that costs set miners their stacks is set over set. Say you hold 77 and your opponent has 88. How often do both of you flop a set? Counting flops from the 48 cards left (excluding both pairs' four cards), the answer is 180 flops out of 17,296, or 1.04%, about 1 in 96. Conditional on you flopping a set, they flop theirs 8.5% of the time (180 out of 2,116).

That 1.04% only applies when they hold 88. For the chance that someone holds a higher pair at all, count pairs: with 77 in your hand, there are 6 higher ranks, each with 6 possible pairs, so 36 of the C(50,2) = 1,225 two-card holdings, or 2.9% for one opponent. Against a deuce the figure doubles, 72 in 1,225 or 5.9%. Set over set is rare, but when it happens the smaller set usually loses everything.

The board matters too: a set on a flop with three to a straight or flush can still be beaten by the right turn and river, so some flops give you fewer free chips than a dry one.

Using it before you call

Keep three questions in order. Is the call small compared with the stacks, around one-fifteenth or less of the effective stack? Is the opponent likely to pay a set off? And do you have a plan for the 88.2% of flops that miss, which is to fold to a bet and take the small loss? If you cannot answer yes to all three, the pair is better folded or reraised.
