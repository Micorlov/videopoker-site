Most people learn a skill by doing it and watching what happens. Video poker is a poor fit for that method, because what happens on any one hand, any one session or even any one month is mostly luck. The maths below, for the standard 9/6 Jacks or Better paytable under correct play, shows why your results cannot tell you whether you are playing well. Something else has to.

Two numbers that describe the game

9/6 Jacks or Better (a full house pays 9, a flush pays 6) returns 99.54% when every hand is played correctly. For each bet, 0.9954 comes back on average, so the shortfall is 0.46% of a bet. Call that the edge.

The second number is the standard deviation, a measure of how far a single hand's result typically lands from that average. For this table it is 4.42 bets per hand. Most of that comes from the royal flush, which pays 800 for a five-coin bet and arrives about once in 40,400 hands. The full working is in What variance actually means at a video poker machine.

Put side by side, the edge is 0.0046 of a bet and the typical swing is 4.42 bets. The swing is roughly 960 times larger.

How each number grows with hands played

The two numbers do not grow at the same rate, and that is the whole story. After n hands of one bet each:

  • Expected loss = 0.0046 × n. It grows in a straight line.
  • One standard deviation = 4.42 × √n. It grows with the square root, so it falls behind slowly.

Both are in bets, so the table holds whatever the bet. About two results in three land within one standard deviation of the expected figure, and about nineteen in twenty within two.

The ratio of swing to expected loss is 960.9 ÷ √n, and it only reaches 1 when n is about 923,000 hands. Before that, luck is the larger of the two.

Why that makes results a poor teacher

Suppose you play with a small leak: say a habit that costs one percentage point of return, 0.01 of a bet per hand. That is more than twice the game's own edge. How many hands before the numbers show it?

To tell the leak apart from luck at two standard deviations, the leak has to reach twice the swing: 0.01 × n = 2 × 4.42 × √n. Solving gives √n = 884, so n is about 781,000 hands. At 400 hands an hour that is nearly 2,000 hours.

So the feedback loop most skills rely on does not exist here. A session in which you broke a rule and won tells you nothing, and neither does a session in which you played perfectly and lost. Both happen routinely. Strategy has to be learned from the strategy itself: which hold has the highest expected value for this deal. Jacks or Better strategy, ranked sets out the order of holds, and the most useful practice is the kind that checks your choice against that order on every deal.

What free practice gives you

Because results cannot tell you how you are doing, the things that do tell you matter more. These features come from the app's own description.

  • Strategy hints. Turn them on and the game points to which cards to hold, so you learn the correct choice as you decide.
  • The paytable on screen. Every variant shows its full paytable at all times, so differences between tables are visible without looking anything up. How to read a video poker paytable explains what to look for.
  • Four games. Jacks or Better, Deuces Wild, Bonus Poker and Double Bonus Poker. They carry different strategies, so switching between them is a way to practise what actually changes.
  • Single hand, Triple Play and Five Play. You hold once and draw across 1, 3 or 5 hands, which shows how multi-hand play spreads results. For more on that, see Five Play: what multi-hand does to your bankroll.
  • Stats saved after every hand, with chips and progress stored on your phone, and the whole game working with no wifi or data.
  • A free rebuy when you run out, so a bad run never ends a session. The chips are free, carry no cash value, and the game offers no real money and no prizes.

The rebuy changes how you can learn. A bad run costs nothing, so you can sit through a few thousand hands and see what a 4.42 swing feels like: stretches where nothing pays, then a cluster of wins, none of which says anything about your play. The bankroll and session length post has the arithmetic behind how long a given run can last.

Over any sensible number of hands, luck outweighs the game's built-in cost by dozens of times. Whether you are playing well shows up in your decisions, not your results, so practise where you can check the decision.

A practice routine that uses the numbers

  1. Learn one game first. Jacks or Better has the shortest hold order.
  2. Use hints until the holds are automatic, then switch them off and see how often your choice matches.
  3. Ignore your net result. Count hold mistakes instead.
  4. Move to a higher-variance game later. Full-pay Deuces Wild has a standard deviation of about 5.1 against 4.42 here, so the swings feel bigger.

The hours then go into the one thing you can improve, the choice on each deal, rather than the outcome you cannot control.
