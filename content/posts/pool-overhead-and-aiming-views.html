Stand at a pool table and look along the cue. You see the cue ball, the object ball and the pocket stacked up the table in one line, and that is what makes aiming possible. Look down on the same shot from above and the line vanishes, but every distance and angle becomes honest. Neither view is better; they answer different questions.

What a view from a foot up does

Take the eye as roughly 12 in above the cloth, which is about right for a player down on the shot. A point on the table at horizontal distance x from the eye is seen at a depression angle of atan(12 / x). Distances running away from you are squashed by roughly the sine of that angle, while distances running across your view keep close to full scale. The table below uses 12 in as a working figure, not a measured standard.

Foreshortening for an eye 12 in above the cloth. Depth scale is the size of a length running away from you relative to the same length running across.
Distance from eyeDepression angleDepth scale
24 in26.6°about 0.45
48 in14.0°about 0.24
96 in7.1°about 0.12

At 96 in, close to the length of a 9-foot table, a stretch of cloth along the line of sight is drawn at about an eighth of the scale of the same stretch across it. That is why a long shot looks like a short one: the far end is crammed into a thin band, and a ball a hand’s width off the line can still look as if it is sitting on it.

What the overhead view does

From straight above, a flat table is a scaled copy of itself. Lengths are in proportion in every direction, angles are the real angles, and two balls 20 in apart look 20 in apart whether they are near you or far away. Nothing hides behind anything else, so a cluster reads as it lies.

What you lose is the sight line. There is no “along the cue” to look down, and you cannot see the height of anything. Where the tip meets the cue ball, high for topspin or low for backspin, is a vertical position, and from above it is invisible. The cue ball spin post covers why that point matters so much.

The cut angle in each view

A cut angle is the angle between the cue ball’s path and the line the object ball must take to the pocket. In plan view it can be read off directly. From behind the cue ball it cannot, because the two lines recede at different rates and foreshortening bends the angle between them. The distortion depends on how far the lines run from your sight line and how far away the object ball is, so a cut that is 30° on the cloth can look like something else from the stance.

The ghost-ball method makes the cut concrete. The ghost ball is the position of the cue ball at the moment of contact, one ball diameter from the object ball’s centre along the line to the pocket. A pool ball is 2.25 in across, so the ghost ball’s centre sits 2.25 in behind the object ball’s, and the contact point on the object ball is half that, 1.125 in from its centre, on the side facing you.

In this view the 30° is simply measured. The sideways gap between the cue ball’s line and the object ball’s centre is 2.25 in × sin 30°, which is 1.125 in, so the cue ball overlaps the object ball by half its width: a half-ball hit. The overlap is 2.25 in × (1 − sin of the angle), which gives these overlaps:

  • 15° cut: about 74% of the ball’s width.
  • 30° cut: 50%.
  • 45° cut: about 29%.
  • 60° cut: about 13%.

On a plan view you can lay a half-diameter off the object ball’s edge and see it. In perspective the ball’s centre is 1.125 in above the cloth, you see mostly its top, and the ghost-ball spot is a point you must imagine on cloth you cannot see well. The ghost-ball post covers the method.

Why the long shot costs most

Move the ghost ball’s centre sideways by 0.1 in and the object ball leaves about 0.1 / 2.25 radians off line, roughly 2.5°. Over 60 in to the pocket that is about 2.7 in sideways, and the pocket geometry post shows how little a pocket mouth forgives.

Now put the same 0.1 in at the far end of a long rail shot, 96 in from the eye. Seen from a foot up, it is a tiny shift in a band squeezed to an eighth of its true depth. The low view gives you the line, but it gives you the least precision exactly where a long shot needs it.

Using both views on one shot

The practical rule is to split the job. Choose first, then aim.

  1. Plan view to choose. Read the cut angle, check the object ball is not blocked, decide which pocket, and see where the cue ball will end up. The position play post depends on exactly this overview.
  2. Aiming view to set. Once the shot is chosen, get the line along which cue ball, ghost ball and object ball are stacked, and fix where the tip will strike.

Plan view decides what to play: shot, pocket, angle, position. Aiming view decides how to play it: line and tip.
