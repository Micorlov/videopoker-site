Both run on steel wheels on steel rails, and from a distance a modern light rail vehicle can look like a short train. The differences are in the rules each one operates under. A main-line train is separated from everything else and kept apart from other trains by signals. A tram often shares space with pedestrians, cyclists and cars, and its driver is expected to stop for whatever appears. Almost every other difference follows from that one.

All numbers below are typical values. They vary from country to country and from one system to the next, so treat them as the usual order of magnitude, not as rules.

Driving on sight, or by signal

A tram driver on a street section drives on sight. They look ahead, and they must be able to stop within the distance they can see. Speeds in streets are low, commonly around 50 km/h (31 mph) or less, and trams are built with strong brakes, including track brakes that clamp onto the rail. An emergency rate of about 2.5 m/s² is a reasonable typical figure.

A main-line train cannot work this way. It is heavy, it has only the grip between steel wheel and steel rail, and braking is gentle: service rates are usually below 1 m/s², and this post uses a cautious round 0.5 m/s² to show the scale. At 100 mph the driver could not stop in the distance they can see. Signals therefore give the driver permission to proceed over a block of line, as explained in how railway signals keep trains apart.

The stopping distance from speed v at constant deceleration a is v² ÷ 2a. For the figures above:

  • Tram, 50 km/h = 13.9 m/s: 13.9² ÷ (2 × 2.5) = about 39 m.
  • Main-line train, 50 mph = 22.35 m/s: 22.35² ÷ (2 × 0.5) = about 500 m.
  • Main-line train, 100 mph = 44.7 m/s: 44.7² ÷ (2 × 0.5) = about 1,998 m, nearly 2 km.

That is why a tram can share a street and a fast train cannot. Level crossings are the place where the two worlds meet, covered in how automatic barriers know a train is coming.

Overhead power: 750 V or 25,000 V

Trams usually take power from an overhead wire at a low voltage, typically 600 to 750 V DC. Low voltage suits a street: wires hang close to buildings, and the equipment on the vehicle is smaller. The cost is current. Power is voltage times current, so a low voltage needs a large current for the same power, and that means thick wires and substations placed fairly close together.

UK main lines that are electrified overhead use 25 kV AC, which is 25,000 V. The ratio is 25,000 ÷ 750, about 33 times, or about 42 times against 600 V. For the same power, the current is about 33 times smaller, so the wire can be lighter and the supply points further apart. High voltage needs large clearances, which is why bridges and tunnels on such lines have to be built or altered for it. Not every main line is the same: some use a third rail at about 750 V DC instead, and other countries use other systems.

Curves and gradients

A main line is laid out for speed. Its curves are gentle, typically hundreds of metres in radius or more, and its gradients are small. A common limit on a heavy railway is around 1 in 100 to 1 in 50, which is 1% to 2%, for reasons set out in why railways follow rivers.

A tram has to follow the street. Tight corners are normal, with minimum radii on the order of 20 to 25 m, and modern vehicles can manage steeper hills, commonly 6% to 8% and in some systems more. They can do so because they are lighter, they often have motors on many axles, and they travel slowly. Wheels still follow the coned-profile idea described in coned wheels: how a train steers itself round a curve, but a tram has to take the corner at a crawl.

Lighter vehicles, lighter track

Main-line trains carry heavy axle loads, typically somewhere around 20 to 25 tonnes per axle for freight and a little less for passenger stock, so the track needs deep ballast, strong rails and robust bridges. A tram is lighter, with axle loads often around 10 tonnes. The track can be built into the road surface with grooved rail, and it is cheaper to put in, which is a large part of why cities choose it.

Light rail sits between the two. It usually has its own right of way for much of its length, higher speeds than a street tram and longer stops, but it may still run on the street in the centre. It is a spectrum, not a category with a sharp edge.
