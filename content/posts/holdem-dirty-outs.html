An out is an unseen card that makes your hand the best hand. The second half of that definition is the one people skip. A card that completes your draw but also completes a better hand for your opponent is called a dirty out, and counting it as a full out makes a draw look stronger than it is. This post shows how to find dirty outs and what they cost, with exact card counts.

What makes an out dirty

A clean out improves your hand and leaves it ahead. A dirty out improves your hand but gives someone else something better, or at least something equal. Two patterns cover most cases:

  1. The flush card. Your straight card is also the third card of a suit on the board. An opponent holding two cards of that suit now has a flush, which beats your straight.
  2. The pairing card. Your card pairs the board. An opponent who already has trips or two pair can turn that into a full house, which beats a flush or a straight.

None of these means the out is worthless. It means the out is only good when your opponent does not hold the hand it helps. You cannot see that hand, so you discount.

A worked example

You hold the 10♠ 9♠. The flop is the 8♥ 7♥ 2♣. Any 6 gives you 6-7-8-9-10 and any jack gives you 7-8-9-10-J, so you have an open-ended straight draw: four sixes and four jacks, 8 outs.

The board has two hearts. If a third heart arrives, anyone holding two hearts has a flush. Two of your eight outs are hearts, the 6♥ and the J♥. If the opponent is on a heart draw, those two cards win you nothing, because the straight they give you loses to the flush they give him. That leaves 6 clean outs.

Count the cost with the two-card formula from the flop to the river: one minus the chance of missing both cards, 1 − C(47 − outs, 2) ÷ C(47, 2). There are 47 unseen cards and C(47, 2) = 1,081 pairs of them.

  • 8 outs: 1 − 741 ÷ 1,081 = 340 ÷ 1,081 = 31.5%, since C(39, 2) = 741.
  • 6 outs: 1 − 820 ÷ 1,081 = 261 ÷ 1,081 = 24.1%, since C(41, 2) = 820.

Two cards cost you more than seven percentage points. Counting them as clean would have overstated the draw by nearly a third.

The same hand on the turn

The turn is the K♦ and nothing has changed in the hearts: the board is 8♥ 7♥ 2♣ K♦, and 46 cards are unseen. With one card to come the chance is simply outs ÷ 46.

  • 8 outs: 8 ÷ 46 = 17.4%.
  • 6 outs: 6 ÷ 46 = 13.0%.

Each out is worth 1 ÷ 46, or about 2.2 points, so every dirty out you remove costs that much.

Now add a price. The pot is 60 and your opponent bets 15. Calling adds 15 to make a pot of 90, so you need 15 ÷ 90 = 16.7%, using the division from the post on pot odds. At 8 outs you clear it narrowly, at 17.4%. At 6 outs you are well short, at 13.0%. The dirty outs turn a call into a fold, which is the whole reason to count them.

Discounting when you are not sure

You rarely know whether the opponent holds hearts. Two practical ways to handle that:

  1. Count by likelihood. If a heart draw is about as likely as not, treat the two heart outs as one: 7 outs, 7 ÷ 46 = 15.2%. That is still under the 16.7% price, so the call remains marginal at best.
  2. Read the betting. A bet that has not slowed down on a two-heart board is more worrying than a check. The more the line fits a flush draw, the nearer to a full discount you should go.

Where the shortcut goes wrong

Discounting is a correction to the count, not to the method. The rule of 2 and 4 still applies once you have the right number of outs: 6 outs on the turn is 12 by the rule of 2, against an exact 13.0%. Two cautions:

  1. Do not discount everything. An out is dirty only if a specific hand that beats you becomes possible. Run through the two patterns above and discount only those cards, not the whole draw.
  2. Do not use the two-card figure for a one-card price. The 24.1% on the flop applies only if you see both cards for one payment. If another bet is likely on the turn, the next-card figure is the honest one.

Before you count outs, ask of each one: if this card arrives, could my opponent have something better because of it? Remove the outs where the answer is yes, then turn what is left into a percentage and compare with the price.
