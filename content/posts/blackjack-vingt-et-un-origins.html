Blackjack is a game of reaching 21 without going over, and the name has nothing to do with that. It comes from a bonus that casinos stopped paying long ago. The history of card games is thin on paperwork, so this post sorts what it says into three kinds: what is documented, what is repeated often but cannot be proved, and what is plainly folklore. It says which is which as it goes.

The oldest mention

The earliest reference people usually cite is in Spanish. Cervantes’ story Rinconete y Cortadillo, published in 1613, has a pair of sharpers in Seville who play veintiuna, “twenty-one”. As the passage is usually read, the ace counts as 1 or 11 and the aim is to reach 21. That is documented. What it does not show is that this game was the direct ancestor of blackjack; a game with the same target is not proof of a single line of descent.

French vingt-et-un

The name the English-speaking world inherited is French: vingt-et-un, twenty-one. The game was fashionable in France in the 18th century. Stories that it was played at the court of Louis XV, or that Napoleon was a fan, are repeated in popular histories. Treat them as folklore: plausible, but resting on retelling, not on a source you can check.

What is on firmer ground is the 19th-century manuals. They describe vingt-et-un, and an English corruption, Van John, turns up in print as well. In the manuals the dealer was usually a player holding the bank, who could take a card or stand as he chose. Accounts of the details differ: in many of them, a natural paid double and the bank passed to whoever held it. The point is that nothing forced this dealer to draw or stand.

The black jack bonus

The English name comes from the American card rooms. The usual account is that, to attract players to a game that had been slow to catch on, houses offered an extra payout on one particular two-card hand: the ace of spades with a black jack, meaning the jack of spades or clubs. The figure most often quoted is 10 to 1. The hand was called a black jack, and the name outlived the bonus. When the bonus was withdrawn, any ace with a ten-value card kept the name.

That account is commonly repeated, and it is the best explanation going. What is not established is the year, the house that began it, or whether 10 to 1 was ever a single standard figure. Be wary of an exact date.

The arithmetic is checkable, and it shows why the bonus was cheap to offer. With a single deck there are 4 aces and 16 ten-value cards, so 64 different ace-plus-ten pairs. Only 2 of them are the ace of spades with a black jack. So the bonus hand was one natural in 32, which is 2 chances in 1,326 pairs of cards, or about 0.15% of hands. If the bonus really was 10 to 1, instead of the 3 to 2 a natural pays now (1.5 times the stake), the extra 8.5 units paid on that hand cost the house about 1.3% of the stake, on average. The case for 3 to 2 as a natural’s payout is in its own post.

What can be dated

Few dates here are firm. These four are, and the timeline shows them in order.

Nevada legalised casino gambling in 1931, which put American card rooms on a licensed footing. In 1956 four US army mathematicians (Baldwin, Cantey, Maisel and McDermott) published a paper on the optimum strategy, and in 1962 Edward Thorp’s Beat the Dealer brought it to the public. That last story is told in its own post, so it is not repeated here.

The rules that survived, and the ones that arrived

Set the older game beside the one the app plays and a pattern shows: the rules that governed the dealer became fixed, and the rules that gave the player choices were added.

  • A fixed dealer. Where the old banker chose, the casino dealer follows a rule. The app’s dealer stands on 17. Removing the dealer’s choice is what makes the game a contest with a known edge, and it is why a dealer’s result can be tabulated, as in the post on dealer bust rates.
  • The natural’s pay. The app pays blackjack at 3 to 2. In the old game the natural’s reward was a double stake or the bank, and the modern 3 to 2 is a casino convention.
  • Doubling and splitting. The app offers hit, stand, double down, split, surrender and insurance. Doubling and splitting are later additions, and I do not have a reliable date for either, so I am not giving one. By the 1950s and 1960s they were part of standard play in Nevada, which is why the strategy studies of those years had to deal with them. See doubling down and splitting pairs.
  • Decks. The game was dealt from a single deck by hand. The app deals from a six-deck shoe. Casinos added decks in response to card counting, and that is usually dated to the 1960s, though there was no single day it happened. What a shoe changes is in the comparison with a single deck.

The name, then, is a leftover. No modern table pays a bonus for the ace of spades with a black jack, yet every ace-and-ten hand is still a black jack.
