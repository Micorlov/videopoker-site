You hold the king, nine, six and two of spades, plus the four of hearts. Keep the four spades, exchange the heart, and you complete the flush if the new card is a spade. How often is that? A shade under one time in five. Four times in five the exchange gives you a hand that is worse than the flush you were hoping for, and that is the normal outcome, not a sign that something went wrong.

Counting the outs

A suit has 13 cards. You hold four of them, so 9 spades are still unseen. These are your outs: the cards that finish the hand.

The deck has 52 cards and you can see five of them, so 47 are unseen. Each is equally likely to be the one you receive. That gives

9 ÷ 47 = 19.15%

and the miss is 38 ÷ 47 = 80.85%, since 47 − 9 = 38. In odds terms that is 38 to 9 against, or about 4.2 to 1. Put the other way round, 47 ÷ 9 = 5.2, so on average a four-card flush completes once in every 5.2 attempts.

Four to a flush, one card to come: 9 outs, 47 unseen cards, 19.15%. It does not depend on which suit you hold or which ranks, only on there being four of one suit in your hand.

How it compares with other one-card draws

Every one-card draw is a count of outs over the same 47. Only the outs change:

  • Open-ended straight draw (four in a row with both ends open, such as 5-6-7-8): eight outs, 8 ÷ 47 = 17.02%.
  • Inside straight draw (one gap, such as 5-6-8-9): four outs, 4 ÷ 47 = 8.51%. Why an inside straight draw is worth exactly half an outside one covers that pair.
  • Two pair to a full house: two cards of each pair's rank remain, so four outs, 8.51%.
  • One named card: a single out, 1 ÷ 47 = 2.13%.

The flush draw therefore sits just above the open-ended straight draw: nine outs against eight. It is also more than double any draw with four outs.

Draws can also overlap. Suppose your four spades are also four in a row, say the 5, 6, 7 and 8 of spades. Any spade completes the flush (9 outs). Any 4 or 9 completes the straight (8 outs), but the 4 and 9 of spades are already counted among the spades, so only six non-spade cards add something new. Total outs: 9 + 6 = 15, and 15 ÷ 47 = 31.91%.

Missing is the usual result

An 80.85% miss rate is easy to nod at and hard to feel. Take five separate hands in which you start with four to a flush and make one exchange each. The chance you miss all five is (38 ÷ 47)5 = 34.5%, so more than a third of the time, five in a row will fail. The chance of at least one flush in those five is 65.5%.

When the unseen count changes

The 47 assumes that the only cards you know are your own five. Any other card you can see removes one from the unseen pile, and the effect depends on what it is. Suppose you can see one extra card that comes from the same deck as the card you will receive:

  • If it is not a spade: 9 outs among 46 unseen, 9 ÷ 46 = 19.57%.
  • If it is a spade: 8 outs among 46 unseen, 8 ÷ 46 = 17.39%.

So a visible card of your suit takes about two points off the draw, and a visible card of another suit adds less than half a point. Cards held by other players that you cannot see change nothing: from your seat they are as unknown as the rest of the deck, so 47 is still the right denominator.

Pricing the draw

If each exchange costs something, the 19.15% has to be set against that cost. Suppose an exchange costs a stake c, you lose it on a miss, and a hit gains G over what you had. The draw breaks even when 9 ÷ 47 × G = 38 ÷ 47 × c, which gives G = (38 ÷ 9) × c = 4.22c. A completed flush must be worth a little over four times the price of the attempt before the exchange is better than doing nothing, ignoring any value the hand already has.

Breaking up a pair to chase a flush also gives up a hand that already has value, which the formula ignores. For the pair side of that comparison, see The odds of improving a pair when you exchange cards. How rare a finished flush is when dealt, and why it outranks a straight, is in Why a flush beats a straight.

Checklist for the table

  1. Count the suit. Four cards of one suit leave 9 of that suit among 47 unseen.
  2. Divide. 9 ÷ 47 is 19.15%. For a straight draw, replace the 9 with 8 or 4.
  3. Expect the miss. Four attempts in five fail, and that is the price of the draw, not a streak.
