Players learn early that the ace-high flush is the nut flush, the best flush there is. That is true, and it is also incomplete. A flush ranks below a full house, and a full house needs only a pair on the board plus the right two cards in an opponent's hand. This post counts those hands on one concrete board, and then counts how often the board pairs at all.

One hand, one board

You hold A♥ 7♥ K♠ 3♣. The board runs 9♥ 9♣ 5♥ 2♥ Q♦. You play the ace and seven of hearts with the three hearts on the board: A-9-7-5-2, the nut flush. No heart hand can beat it, and no straight flush is possible on these three hearts.

The board is paired, though, so you must now look at a different ranking. A full house needs three cards of one rank and two of another. Because exactly two hole cards play, an opponent has to build it from two of their cards and three from the board. On this board that happens in three ways.

  1. One nine plus a card that pairs the board. The 9♦ or 9♠ with a 5, a 2 or a queen. Nines on the board and one in hand make trips, and the matching card makes the pair: 9-9-9-5-5, for example.
  2. A pocket pair that matches a board card. A pair of fives, twos or queens in hand, with the single board card of that rank, makes three of a kind, and the board nines supply the pair: 5-5-5-9-9.
  3. Both remaining nines. That is four of a kind, which beats everything you can hold here.

A single nine alone is not enough. A nine with an irrelevant card gives trips and nothing more, which loses to your flush.

Counting the hands that beat you

You can see nine cards: your four and the five on the board. That leaves 43 unseen, and an opponent holds four of them, so there are C(43,4) = 123,410 possible opposing hands. I enumerated all of them, picked each hand's best five-card combination using exactly two hole cards and three board cards, and counted what beat the flush.

The total is 18,634 hands, or 15.1%. Four of a kind is the easy one to check by hand: both remaining nines plus any two of the other 41 cards is C(41,2) = 820. The other two rows overlap in the raw counting (a hand can hold a pocket pair and a nine), so the enumeration assigned each hand to one row only: four of a kind first, then a pocket pair, then a nine with a pairing card.

For one opponent that is about one hand in 6.6. The pot rarely stays heads-up, so I also dealt 30,000 random hands to each field size. At two opponents, someone beat the flush about 29% of the time, and at three about 40%. These are simulated, so treat them as good to a point or so, but the direction is not in doubt: every added opponent is another four cards that might fit the board.

How often the board pairs

All of this matters only when the board pairs, so the next question is how often it does. A board has no pair when every card has a different rank. The first card is free, the second must avoid its rank (48 of 51 cards), the third must avoid both (44 of 50), and so on.

  • Flop, three cards: 1 − (48 ÷ 51 × 44 ÷ 50) = 17.2% contain a pair or trips.
  • Turn, four cards: 1 − (48 ÷ 51 × 44 ÷ 50 × 40 ÷ 49) = 32.4%.
  • River, five cards: 1 − (48 ÷ 51 × 44 ÷ 50 × 40 ÷ 49 × 36 ÷ 48) = 49.3%.

By the river, then, about half of all boards are paired. A flush you hold on the flop is more often safe than not, but the safety wears off street by street. A flush that is good on the flop on an unpaired board can turn into a loser when the turn or river pairs a board card, even though your own hand has not changed.

The same flush, an unpaired board

Change the board to 9♥ 6♣ 5♥ 2♥ Q♦. Your A-high flush is unchanged, but no rank appears twice, so no opponent can make a full house with two hole cards and three board cards. The 15.1% from the table falls to zero. On this board, with no straight flush possible either, the ace-high flush really is the nuts. The hand is identical; the board decides whether it is the nuts.

The same logic applies below the nuts. The second-best flush has two ways to lose on a paired board: to the ace-high flush and to any full house.

Rules to take to the table

  1. Look for a pair before you look at your flush. If a rank appears twice, ask which hands make the full house before you call the flush a certainty.
  2. Count the cards that fit. Here that is two nines and three each of fives, twos and queens. A board with more unseen cards that pair it gives opponents more combinations.
  3. Remember the exactly-two rule. One nine in an opponent's hand is not a danger unless the other hole card also matches the board. This keeps the count lower than it first looks.
  4. Re-check on every street. A river that pairs the board is a different board.
