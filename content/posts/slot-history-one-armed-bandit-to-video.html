A modern slot is a screen, a random number generator and a table of results. Its ancestor was a cast-iron box with a lever, three drums and a spring. Between the two there are four well-documented steps, and each one answers the same engineering question: how many different outcomes can the machine have? This post follows those steps with the arithmetic. The Liberty Bell, late 1890s

The machine usually credited as the first successful slot is the Liberty Bell, built by Charles Fey in San Francisco in the late 1890s (c. 1898 is the date most often given). It had three spinning reels, and the lever on its side earned it the nickname “one-armed bandit”. The reels carried horseshoes, diamonds, spades, hearts and a Liberty Bell, with ten positions on each reel. The top result was three bells.

The number of outcomes is simple to compute. Ten positions on each of three reels gives 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 combinations. If each of the 1,000 is equally likely, any one named combination turns up once in 1,000 spins on average.

The payout was mechanical too. Everything, from the reel stops to the coin tray, was a physical part, so the maker could not change the odds without cutting new metal.

Fruit and the 22-stop limit

In the early twentieth century slot makers moved to fruit symbols, and the three-reel layout became the standard. A mechanical reel can only hold so many stops. A figure widely quoted for traditional machines is 22 stops per reel, and it gives a ceiling of 22 × 22 × 22 = 10,648 combinations on three reels. That is about ten times the Liberty Bell, but it is still small. Symbols also had to be big enough to read, so the practical limit sat close to that.

Money Honey, 1963

The next step was electrical, not mathematical. In 1963 Bally introduced Money Honey, widely described as the first fully electromechanical slot. Its reels still turned and stopped on physical positions, but electricity drove the mechanism, and it had a hopper that paid out coins automatically. Before this, a big win meant an attendant with a bucket. The machine’s odds were still set by its reel stops, so the 10,648 ceiling stood.

The first video slot, 1976

In 1976 the Fortune Coin Company in Las Vegas showed what is generally accepted as the first video slot. The reels were pictures on a modified television set. A picture has no stops to wear out, so in principle it could show any number of positions, and a computer chose the result. For the first time the symbols were no longer tied to metal. In practice the early video machine kept to the familiar three-reel pattern, because that was what players recognised.

The virtual reel, 1984

The change that mattered most for the odds was a patent. In 1984 Inge Telnaes was granted a US patent describing a virtual reel: the random number generator picks from a long list of virtual stops, and a table maps each virtual stop to a position that is actually shown. The physical reel, or the picture of one, stays small. The number of outcomes behind it does not.

Here is a made-up example. Take a reel with 22 visible stops and one jackpot symbol. On a mechanical reel the chance of that symbol is 1 ÷ 22, about 4.5%. Now give the reel 64 virtual stops, and map just one of them to the jackpot symbol. The chance is 1 ÷ 64, or 1.56%. On three reels the jackpot line comes up with probability 1 in 643 = 1 in 262,144, instead of 1 in 223 = 1 in 10,648. That is roughly 24.6 times rarer, with the same picture on the screen.

Each step raised the ceiling on outcomes: 1,000 on the Liberty Bell, 10,648 on a 22-stop mechanical reel, and any number you like once the reel is a table in software.

From three reels to ways to win

Once the result is chosen by software, nothing limits the layout either. Video slots moved to five reels, several rows and many paylines, and later to counting ways instead of lines. A check you can do

Multiply the stops on each reel of any three-reel machine you read about. Ten per reel gives 1,000; 22 per reel gives 10,648; 64 gives 262,144. Then ask which of those is the visible reel and which is the draw behind it. Since 1984 they have not had to be the same number.
