A safety is a shot played to leave your opponent in a bad position rather than to pot a ball. You make a legal shot, but you choose where the cue ball and the object balls finish: hidden behind a ball, tight to a cushion, or a long way from anything they can hit. For a hard pot it is often the stronger choice, and a few multiplications show why.

What makes a safety legal

A safety still has to be a legal shot, otherwise it is just a foul. Under WPA rules, the cue ball must first contact a legal ball: one of your own group in 8-ball once groups are set, or the lowest-numbered ball on the table in 9-ball. After that contact, one of two things must happen: a ball is pocketed, or any ball, the cue ball included, reaches a cushion.

That second condition is the one safeties live on. You do not need to pot anything. You need the cue ball or an object ball to touch a cushion after the first contact. Fail to do both and the shot is a foul, usually giving your opponent the cue ball in hand anywhere on the table. House rules vary: many bar rules limit ball in hand, or give two visits instead, so agree the rules before you start. The 8-ball rules post and the 9-ball rules post cover the full foul lists.

The three common types

  1. Hiding behind a ball. The cue ball finishes with another ball between it and the object ball your opponent needs to hit. They have no straight line and must kick off a cushion, which is hard to do accurately.
  2. Distance. You send the object ball to the far end of the table, or tight to a cushion, and leave the cue ball at the opposite end. Even with a clear view, a long, thin shot is a poor one to be forced into.
  3. Snookering. A snooker blocks the cue ball's direct path to every legal ball with balls that are not legal, such as your opponent's group or the 8 ball. It is the hiding safety taken further: the whole target set is covered, not one ball.

All three make your opponent's best option a low-percentage one.

The expected-value comparison

A fair way to compare a pot with a safety is to ask which gives you the better chance of winning the rack. The figures below are illustrative assumptions, not measured results. They show how the reasoning works; your own numbers will differ.

  • If you pot the ball and have a clear run, you win the rack 80% of the time.
  • If you miss and leave an easy out, you win only 10% of the time, because your opponent usually runs the table.
  • A good safety leaves your opponent a hard shot, and from there you win 40% of the time.

Suppose you pot this shot 30% of the time. Then the pot is worth

0.30 × 80% + 0.70 × 10% = 24% + 7% = 31%

against 40% for the safety. The safety is better by 9 points even though it never pots anything.

Where the pot takes over

Call the chance of potting p. The pot is worth 10% + 70% × p, since the miss gives 10% and a pot adds 70 points on top of that. Set that equal to the safety's 40% and p = 30 ÷ 70 = 42.9%. Below that, play safe; above it, shoot.

The other rows use the same sum: 16% + 8% = 24% at 20%, 32% + 6% = 38% at 40%, 40% + 5% = 45% at 50%, and 56% + 3% = 59% at 70%.

What a miss leaves matters more than the pot

The result depends heavily on the 10% figure. It assumed that a miss hands over an easy out. Change that one assumption: if a miss leaves your opponent only a hard shot, and you still win 30% of the time from there, the pot is worth

0.30 × 80% + 0.70 × 30% = 24% + 21% = 45%

That beats the safety's 40% at the same 30% pot rate. The break-even drops to 30% + 50% × p = 40%, so p = 20%. The same pot goes from wrong to right without the shot changing.

Before a hard pot, ask what the table looks like if you miss. A pot you make 30% of the time is a good shot if the miss leaves a hard shot, and a poor one if the miss leaves an easy out. The pot success rate is only half the sum.

Reading the situation at the table

Three questions cover most decisions.

  1. Where does the object ball go if I miss? A miss that leaves it near a pocket, or opens a cluster for your opponent, is expensive.
  2. Where does the cue ball finish? A cue ball left close to a cushion with no line to a legal ball is the start of a safety. One left in the open is not.
  3. Can I make the safety legal? A ball must reach a cushion after the first contact, or the foul gives them the table.

Speed and spin decide whether the cue ball stops behind the blocking ball, so the cue-ball spin post is a good place to start.
