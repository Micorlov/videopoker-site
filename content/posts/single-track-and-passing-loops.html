Two tracks cost far more than one to build and maintain. A railway with little traffic therefore often has a single track, and trains in both directions share it. That works only because trains never meet between stations. They meet at a passing loop, a short stretch of second track where one train waits and the other goes by. How many trains the line can carry follows from simple division.

The rule: one train in a section

Take the stretch of single track between two places where trains can pass, and call it a section. The rule is that only one train may be in a section at a time. If two entered from opposite ends, neither could pass the other, and one would have to reverse a long way.

The earlier post on railway signals and blocks covered following trains. Single track adds the opposite problem, and the same idea answers it: keep a section empty until the train in it has left.

A worked capacity example

These are the assumptions, chosen for clean arithmetic rather than taken from any real line:

  • Two stations, A and B, are 20 km apart on one track.
  • Every train runs at a constant 60 km/h, which is 1 km a minute.
  • Stops, turnaround time and speed limits through the points are ignored.
  • The trains are short enough to fit in any loop.
  • Trains alternate: one from A, then one from B, then one from A again.

Without a loop. The section is 20 km, so a train needs 20 ÷ 1 = 20 minutes to cross it. Nothing else can enter until it has left. The train from A takes 20 minutes, then the train from B takes the next 20. One of each direction therefore uses 40 minutes of the section, and:

60 minutes ÷ 40 minutes = 1.5 trains an hour in each direction, or 3 in total

The section's running time sets the limit. Faster trains help only in proportion: at 120 km/h the crossing takes 10 minutes, the cycle takes 20, and the limit rises to 3 each way.

With a loop at the midpoint. The line now has two 10 km sections of 10 minutes each. Send a train from A and a train from B at the same moment. Each takes 10 minutes to reach the loop. There the two trains meet, and each uses its own side of the loop to pass. For the next 10 minutes each runs the second half. After 20 minutes both have arrived, both sections are empty, and the next pair can leave:

60 minutes ÷ 20 minutes = 3 trains an hour in each direction

One loop doubled the capacity. In this simple model the cycle time is twice the running time of the longest section. The line is 20 km, so the cycle is 2 × 20 = 40 minutes without a loop, and 2 × 10 = 20 minutes with one. Three loops, spaced evenly, would give four 5 km sections, a cycle of 10 minutes, and 6 trains an hour each way. Real lines gain less than that, since trains must slow for the points and must stop and wait while their opposite number arrives. The answer of 3 is a ceiling, not a timetable.

Keeping the rule: staffs and tokens

Before track circuits and electric signals, the rule needed something physical to enforce it. The classic answer was the train staff: a single object, such as a wooden baton, kept for one section. A driver could enter the section only while carrying the staff, and since there was only one staff, there could only be one train. The driver handed it in at the far end, and it had to be carried back for the next train going the other way.

The weakness is plain from the arithmetic above. If two trains must run the same way in a row, the second has nothing to carry. Railways added a ticket system for this case: the staff was shown to the driver, who was given a written ticket to run, and the last train took the staff itself.

Later, token instruments at each end of the section solved it. The signalmen at each end are linked by wire, and the instruments are interlocked, so that one token can be drawn out at a time. Several tokens can sit in the machines, so trains can follow one another, but until the one in the section is returned, no token can be released for a train from either end. The principle did not change: only the train that holds the authority may be in the section.

Why a loop is a design choice

A loop must be long enough for the longest train that will use it, and it is built where it does most good. On the example line the midpoint is best, since it cuts the longest section to the smallest. If the loop were 5 km from A, the sections would be 5 km and 15 km, the longest would still be 15 minutes, and the cycle would be 30 minutes, giving 2 trains an hour each way instead of 3. The same money spent in the wrong place buys a third of the gain.

What to take from it

  • A section holds one train, so its running time, doubled for the return, sets the cycle.
  • Trains an hour in each direction = 60 ÷ cycle time. Here, 60 ÷ 40 = 1.5.
  • A loop halves the longest section only when it is placed at the middle. At the 5 km point the cycle is 30 minutes, not 20.
  • Staffs and tokens enforce the rule physically: no token, no entry.
