An engine does not make power from volume, it makes it from the mass of air it manages to burn fuel with. Each intake stroke fills the cylinder with a fixed volume, but how many oxygen molecules fit depends on how crowded the air is. That crowding is density. This post works through the physics with numbers you can check, as real-world background rather than a description of any game.

An engine is an air pump

Fuel only releases energy if it has oxygen to burn with, and the engine can only burn as much fuel as the air it takes in will support. A naturally aspirated engine gulps roughly the same volume of air each cycle whatever the weather. So the power it can make scales, to a first approximation, with the mass in that volume. Denser air means more mass, more oxygen, more fuel burned and more power. It is the same reason a pound of boost adds power: a turbocharger or supercharger simply packs more air into the same cylinders.

The ideal gas law does the work

For air at ordinary conditions the ideal gas law gives density as ρ = P ÷ (R × T). Here P is pressure, T is absolute temperature in kelvin and R is the specific gas constant for air, about 287 J/(kg·K). Because R is fixed, at a constant pressure density is inversely proportional to absolute temperature. Celsius will not do here: you have to add 273.15 first.

Take a hot evening and a cold night at the same pressure:

  • 35 °C is 308.15 K.
  • 5 °C is 278.15 K.
  • The density ratio is 308.15 ÷ 278.15 = 1.108.

The cold air holds about 11% more mass in every litre. A 30 °C drop buys only 11% because the scale starts at absolute zero, not at freezing. Across the realistic range of the chart below, -5 °C is 1.149 and 40 °C is 0.984, taking 35 °C as 1.000.

From density to elapsed time

If power tracks air mass, 11% more air means roughly 11% more power. That is a first approximation, so treat 11% as a ceiling, not a promise. The standard rule of thumb then links power to elapsed time. For a given weight, ET varies roughly with the cube root of power, so ET is proportional to 1 ÷ (power)1/3. It is an approximation: it ignores traction, gearing and drag, but it is the figure racers carry in their heads.

Apply it. The cube root of 1.108 is about 1.035, so the car runs about 1 ÷ 1.035 = 0.966 of its previous time, a gain of roughly 3.4%. A car that runs 10.00 seconds on the hot evening might run about 9.66 on the cold night. A third of a second is a long way on a time slip; reading a drag racing time slip shows where it would appear.

Pressure and altitude

Density also scales with pressure, and pressure falls with height. In the standard atmosphere, sea-level pressure is 101.325 kPa at 15 °C, and the pressure at 1,000 m is about 89.9 kPa, around 11% lower. Allowing for the cooler air at that height, density is roughly 9% lower. Denver sits at about 1,600 m, where standard-atmosphere density is roughly 15% below sea level. With the cube-root rule, a naturally aspirated car losing 15% of its power would run about 5% slower: a 10.00 becomes roughly 10.5. That is the standard-day figure, with no weather on top.

Humidity works against you

It seems backwards that damp air should be thinner, but the molar masses explain it. Dry air averages about 29 g/mol. Water vapour, H2O, is 18. Every water molecule that enters a given volume displaces a heavier nitrogen or oxygen molecule, so the mixture weighs less. It also takes up room that oxygen would have filled, so less oxygen reaches the cylinder.

The effect is small beside temperature. At 35 °C and 60% relative humidity, water vapour is about 3% of the air by pressure, and the density falls by about 1%. It always works against you, and it is largest on hot, muggy days.

Density altitude, the one number

Racers do not juggle three variables. They use density altitude: the altitude in the standard atmosphere at which the air would have the density you have now. A weather station computes it from temperature, pressure and humidity, and the tuner reads it off before deciding on a jet change or a timing map. A rule of thumb is that each degree Celsius above the standard temperature adds about 120 ft of density altitude. The 30 °C gap between our two evenings is therefore about 3,600 ft, or 1,100 m, and the standard-atmosphere figure above says 1,000 m takes roughly 9% off the density. That is close to the 11% we found.

It is a rule of thumb, not an exact conversion, but it gives one number to compare between days and tracks. Hot, humid, high ground gives a high density altitude and a slower car. Cold, dry, near sea level gives a low one, sometimes even a negative one, and a quicker car. More power also changes how hard the tyres work, which is where launch RPM comes in.

What to take away

  • Power follows air mass, and density is pressure divided by (R × absolute temperature).
  • 35 °C against 5 °C is 308.15 ÷ 278.15 = 1.108, about 11% more air in the cold.
  • Cube-root rule: about 3.4% off the ET, so a 10.00 runs about 9.66. An approximation.
  • Altitude, and to a lesser degree humidity, cost density; density altitude folds all three into one number.
