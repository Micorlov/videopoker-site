Take five cards from a 52-card deck and the most likely made hand is a single pair. Not a close call: 1,098,240 of the 2,598,960 possible hands are exactly one pair, which is 42.26%. Only hands with nothing at all are more common. That matters when a dealer builds a hand face up, because every card that lands is a small update against a distribution you can know in advance.

Counting one pair

The 2,598,960 is 52 × 51 × 50 × 49 × 48 divided by 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1, with order ignored. Exactly one pair means two cards of one rank and three cards of three other, different ranks. Build it in four choices:

  1. The rank of the pair: 13 choices.
  2. Which two suits the pair uses: C(4,2) = 6 choices.
  3. The three other ranks: they must be different from each other and from the pair, so C(12,3) = 220 choices.
  4. A suit for each of those three cards: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 choices.

Multiply: 13 × 6 × 220 × 64 = 1,098,240. Divide by 2,598,960 and you get 0.4226, or 42.26%. Because the three side cards must be different ranks from each other and from the pair, the count cannot include two pair, trips, a full house or four of a kind. A flush needs five different ranks, so it is excluded too.

The rest of the distribution

Sorting all 2,598,960 hands the same way gives nine groups once the straight flushes are joined together.

High card is 1,302,540 hands, 50.12%. One pair is 1,098,240, 42.26%. Two pair is 123,552, 4.75%. Three of a kind is 54,912, 2.11%. Then the straight at 10,200 hands (0.392%), the flush at 5,108 (0.197%), the full house at 3,744 (0.144%), four of a kind at 624 (0.024%) and straight flushes, royals included, at 40 (0.0015%). The nine rows add back to 2,598,960. The full frequency table shows where each of these comes from, and why a flush is scarcer than a straight.

Pair or nothing is nearly a coin flip

High card is 1,302,540 of 2,598,960 hands. Everything else, a pair or better, is 2,598,960 − 1,302,540 = 1,296,420 hands, which is 49.88%. Against 50.12% for nothing at all, that is as near to even as a card distribution gets.

A pair, at 42.26%, is by far the most common made hand. Anything stronger, two pair or better, is 198,180 hands, only 7.63% of the total.

What beating one pair needs

Against a dealer pair you need one of two things. Either you hold a higher pair, or you hold two pair or better. A pair of one rank is 84,480 hands (6 × 220 × 64), which is 3.25% of all hands, and it is the same for every rank. That makes the dealer’s rank the thing that sets how much you must beat, using an ordinary deal and the usual ranking of hands:

  • Dealer pair of aces: no pair is higher, so only two pair or better wins outright, 7.63% of hands.
  • Dealer pair of nines: five ranks are higher, 5 × 3.25% = 16.25%, plus the 7.63%, which is 23.9%.
  • Dealer pair of twos: twelve ranks are higher, 12 × 3.25% = 39.01%, plus 7.63%, which is 46.6%.

These ignore kickers, which settle a tie between equal pairs (the tie-break rules cover how), so each figure is slightly low. They also treat your five cards as an ordinary deal, which is not quite true when the dealer’s cards are already out of the deck. That is the effect described in card removal, and it moves the figures by a point or so, not by enough to change the ordering.

Same type of hand, very different strength: a pair of aces is beaten about one hand in thirteen, a pair of twos nearly one in two.

One pair is 42.26% and two pair is 4.75%. Two pair is rarer by a factor of 1,098,240 ÷ 123,552 = 8.9, so nearly nine times. It needs two matched ranks and a fifth card matching neither, and there are far fewer ways to do that.

Visible versus hidden

With a hidden dealer hand you can only use the totals above: roughly half the time it ends as nothing, otherwise most likely a pair. A face-up hand lets you ask what the cards still to come can add. The store listing says you can double your stake against the house after the dealer’s third card, and that a win on that doubled stake pays 3 to 1. At that point three dealer cards are showing, so you can read them.

Three cards show a pair or better 17.2% of the time (one minus 52 × 48 × 44 over 52 × 51 × 50). If they are three different ranks, 9 of the 49 unseen cards match one of them and 40 do not, so two more cards miss all three ranks with probability (40 × 39) / (49 × 48) = 66.3%. That leaves at least a 33.7% chance of finishing with a pair or better by matching a rank, before counting straights and flushes. So an unpaired three-card start is a clear underdog to pair up. These assume an ordinary 52-card deck, which the listing does not say, so treat them as a guide.

The listing says bailing recovers half of your committed chips, so a dealer who has already paired high makes staying in dearer, and three scattered low cards make it cheaper. The post on bailing out covers that sum. The distribution above is the prior, and each dealer card is the evidence.
