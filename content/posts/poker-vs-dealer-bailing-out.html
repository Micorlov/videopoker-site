In Poker vs Dealer you can walk away from a hand part-way through. Bailing out, in the listing's words, recovers half of your committed chips. It feels like an admission of defeat, and that feeling is the problem: the decision is arithmetic, and the arithmetic gives you a win-probability threshold you can compute before you act.

What the listing says, and what it leaves out

The store listing gives you three choices on a hand: hold, pay an ante to forge a card by exchanging it for a fresh one, or bail and recover half of your committed chips. That is all it says about bailing. It does not say how many chips a hand commits, how large the prizes are, or what happens to the table pot when you bail. So the sums below use symbols and round example numbers, not the app's own amounts, and they treat the hand as one contest with one prize. Check the in-game rules and payouts for the real figures. The chips are play money with no cash value, so this is a question of playing well, not of winning anything real.

Setting the two options equal

Let C be the chips you have committed. Let W be the chips you would win on top, if you play on and win. Let p be your chance of winning. Bailing returns C ÷ 2, so you are down C ÷ 2 whatever happens next. Playing on has two outcomes: you win W with chance p, or you lose all of C with chance 1 − p.

  • Bail out: −C ÷ 2.
  • Play on: p × W − (1 − p) × C.

The two are equal when p × W − (1 − p) × C = −C ÷ 2. Multiply out the bracket: p × W − C + p × C = −C ÷ 2. Move the plain C across: p × (W + C) = C ÷ 2. Divide through:

Break-even p = C ÷ (2 × (C + W)), and you should bail when your chance of winning is below it.

Three pot sizes, three thresholds

Take C = 100 each time and change only the prize.

  • W = 50: 100 ÷ (2 × 150) = 33.3%. A small prize makes playing on less attractive, so you need a third of the time to justify it.
  • W = 100 (even money): 100 ÷ (2 × 200) = 25%.
  • W = 300: 100 ÷ (2 × 400) = 12.5%. A big prize forgives a long shot.

The even-money case is the one to remember. Playing on is worth 200 × p − 100, which is −100 at 0%, −50 at 25%, 0 at 50%, +50 at 75% and +100 at 100%. Bailing is worth −50 throughout, so the lines cross at 25%. The figure below shows that case.

The rule in one sentence: bail out only when your chance of winning is below half your committed chips divided by committed chips plus prize. The dealer in Poker vs Dealer builds a hand one face-up card at a time, so your estimate of p can change as each card arrives.

Which hands fall below the line

Estimating p takes an assumption the listing does not confirm: that the dealer ends with five cards, drawn from the cards you cannot see. On that basis I counted every possible dealer hand, with ties not counted as wins. Against a dealer with nothing yet showing:

  • 7-5-4-3-2, the lowest possible hand: 0%. Bail at any prize.
  • K-9-6-4-2, no pair: 19.7%. Below 25% and below 33.3%, so bail at even money or less. Above 12.5%, so play on if the prize is 300.
  • A-J-8-5-2, no pair: 38.2%. Above all three thresholds, so play on.
  • A pair of twos: 50.0%.
  • A pair of eights: 70.3%.

The visible dealer cards change these. If the dealer shows ace, king and queen with two cards to come, K-9-6-4-2 falls to 0%, because the dealer already holds ace high. If the dealer shows a pair of kings, every hand below a pair of kings falls to 0%, and two pair, 4s and 3s, still wins 76.5%. Count what the dealer needs, not what your hand looks like. The same counting underlies card removal: the cards you can see are not available to the dealer.

The mistake: bailing out of hands that merely look lost

The common error is bailing with a low pair. A pair of twos looks poor because it is the lowest pair, but a pair beats every hand with no pair, and the dealer starts with no pair more often than not. It wins half the time against a dealer with nothing showing, and 56.3% against one showing ace, king and queen. Even at 25% that is twice what is needed. Bailing there gives up a hand worth well above −50 to lock in −50.

The reverse error is less common but real: clinging to a hand because you have already committed chips. The chips are gone either way; the threshold only asks about the chance from here. If you are looking at 0%, as with K-9-6-4-2 under an ace-high dealer, half back is the best available result.

A short routine. Before you bail:

  1. Work out the threshold with C ÷ (2 × (C + W)). It is never above 50%, and for even money it is 25%.
  2. Estimate your chance against what the dealer shows, using what the dealer still needs.
  3. Bail only if your chance is below the threshold. A hand that looks bad but is above it is a play-on.

For the same trade-off as a payout rather than a refund, break-even odds for a 3-to-1 payout converts a prize into a win rate, and picking a kicker covers the other choice the dealer round asks of you. Both are decisions you can price before you make them, on Poker vs Dealer.
