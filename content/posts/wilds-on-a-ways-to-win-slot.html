A ways game counts a symbol by multiplying how many times it shows on each reel, working from the left until a reel has none. A wild enters that sum in the simplest possible way: on the reel where it lands, it counts as one more copy of the symbol being counted. Everything below follows from that rule, which is the general case rather than any one game's pay table. The basics of the count are in what 243 ways to win means.

One wild, one extra copy

Take a symbol, say a fish, with these counts on reels 1 to 5: 2, 1, 2, 1, 1. The ways are 2 × 1 × 2 × 1 × 1 = 4, all of them five-of-a-kind. Now put a wild on one reel, in a cell that was not already a fish. That reel's count goes up by one, and every other reel stays the same. The total changes by the factor (n + 1) ÷ n, where n is the old count on that reel.

  • On a reel with one fish, the count goes from 1 to 2, so the ways double.
  • On a reel with two fish, it goes from 2 to 3, so the ways rise by half.

Working it through for each reel in turn:

The sums: reel 1 is 3 × 1 × 2 × 1 × 1 = 6, reel 2 is 2 × 2 × 2 × 1 × 1 = 8, reel 3 is 2 × 1 × 3 × 1 × 1 = 6, reel 4 is 2 × 1 × 2 × 2 × 1 = 8 and reel 5 is 2 × 1 × 2 × 1 × 2 = 8. The scarcer the symbol is on a reel, the more a wild adds there. A wild beside two fish adds 50%, a wild on a reel with only one fish adds 100%.

The count on any reel is capped at 3, because a reel has only three cells. Three on every reel gives 35 = 243 ways.

The wild that fills a gap

The multiplier is the smaller of the wild's two jobs. The larger one shows up when a reel has none of the symbol at all, because a count of zero ends the run.

Suppose the fish counts are 1, 1, 2, 0, 1. The run stops at reel 3, since reel 4 is empty, so the result is a three-of-a-kind with 1 × 1 × 2 = 2 ways. The fish on reel 5 counts for nothing.

Now let a wild land on reel 4. That reel's count is 1 instead of 0, the run carries on, and it reaches reel 5. The ways are 1 × 1 × 2 × 1 × 1 = 2, still two, but they are now five-of-a-kind rather than three. The number of ways did not move at all, and the result got better, because the pay table steps up with the length of the run. A wild on reel 5 instead would have changed nothing here, since the run would still have ended at reel 3.

This is why a wild in the middle of the grid feels so much more powerful than one at the edge, even though the multiplier is the same size on every reel. A middle reel is where a gap does the most damage, and the wild repairs it.

A wild has two effects. It multiplies the ways by (n + 1) ÷ n on its reel, and when n is 0 it keeps the run going. The second effect is the one that changes the pay.

Reel 1 and full-wild reels

The first reel works as a gate: a symbol missing from it scores zero, whatever the other reels show. A wild on reel 1 counts for every symbol at once, so each symbol that was absent from that reel now has a count of 1 and can start a run. Each symbol is still worked out on its own, one wild at a time.

The extreme case is a reel that is wild in all three cells. Its count is 3 for every symbol. A symbol with counts of 2 on the other four reels then has 2 × 2 × 3 × 2 × 2 = 48 ways, and five wild reels give 243 for everything.

Real games add rules on top: which reels can carry wilds, whether a wild pays by itself, whether it stands in for every symbol.
