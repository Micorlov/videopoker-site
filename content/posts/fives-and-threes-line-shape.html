A standard double-six set has 28 tiles, and 7 of them are doubles. In some dominoes games the first double played becomes a spinner, and tiles can then be added to its sides as well as its ends, so the line grows in four directions. Fives and Threes does not work like that. The line is one chain with exactly two ends from the first tile to the last, and every score in the game is worked out from those two ends.

A double lies across, but it does not branch

In the pub game a double is laid crossways, at right angles to the line, so you can see it is a double. Nothing may be played off its long sides, and the next tile goes on the end of the line as it would after any other tile. The line has two ends before the double is laid and two ends after it.

What a double does change is its value as an end. It shows its number on both halves, and both count. A 4-4 at the end of the line makes that end worth 8, not 4. The listing for Fives and Threes Dominoes says the same: a double at an open end counts both its spots.

One sum, always two terms

Because the line has two ends, the score for any move is the same calculation: add the two end values, then see whether the total divides by 3, by 5, or by both. A total that divides by 3 scores the total divided by 3. A total that divides by 5 scores the total divided by 5. A total of 15 divides by both and scores 5 + 3 = 8 points. There is never a third end to add.

A line worked through

Start with a 6-3 as the opening tile. I leave the opener’s own count aside and follow the three plays after it. Here is the position after the second of them.

Step by step, with the two end totals each time:

  1. Lay 3-3 crossways on the 3 end. The ends are 6 (the open half of the 6-3) and the double at 3 + 3 = 6. The total is 12, which divides by 3, so it scores 12 ÷ 3 = 4 points. It does not divide by 5.
  2. Lay 6-4 on the left, matching the 6. The ends are now 4 and 6. The total is 10, which divides by 5 and not by 3, so it scores 10 ÷ 5 = 2 points. This is the position in the figure.
  3. Lay 3-2 on the double, matching its 3. The double stops being an end. The ends are 4 and 2, the total is 6, and it scores 6 ÷ 3 = 2 points.

Notice what the double did in step 1. A plain 3 at that end would have made the total 9, which scores 3. The double turned the odd end 3 into an even 6, and the total became 12, which scores 4. It was still a sum of two numbers throughout.

You can try lines like this in Fives and Threes Dominoes, where the table shows the open-end totals and the points scored for each move.

Odd, even, and why 15 needs a double

A double at an end is always worth an even number: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 or 12. A plain end is worth its single number, 0 to 6. That gives a checkable fact. With no double at either end, the largest possible total is 6 + 6 = 12. So 15, the only total that scores both ways, cannot be made without a double at one of the ends.

The parity matters here too. 15 is odd, and a double end is even, so the other end must be odd. That leaves two combinations: a 6-6 end (12) with a 3 at the other end, or a 5-5 end (10) with a 5. The post on why 15 scores best counts how rarely those arise. The same parity logic works on smaller totals: a double end and an even number at the other end give an even total, which can only be 6, 10, 12 or 18, never 9 or 15.

How many tiles fit at an end

Each number from 0 to 6 sits on exactly seven tiles in the set, the double included. If an end needs a 6, then one of those seven tiles is already the end itself, so at most six can still be played there. In the line above the left end needs a 6. The unplayed tiles carrying a 6 are 6-0, 6-1, 6-2, 6-4, 6-5 and 6-6, so six. The right end needs a 3, and both the 6-3 and the 3-3 are on the table, so only five remain: 3-0, 3-1, 3-2, 3-4 and 3-5. That is 11 of the 26 tiles not yet played that fit one end or the other, and fewer once you allow for those in your own hand. The counting tiles post uses the same seven-tile rule to work out what an opponent cannot hold.

The result is a small number of legal plays. Your tile has to carry one of two numbers, so even a hand of seven will often contain only a few that fit. In the app you select a tile and then choose an end, and ends that cannot accept the tile are marked. If nothing fits, you draw from the boneyard until something does, or pass when it is empty.

What the two-end shape means for play

With two ends there are two numbers to keep in mind, and a good turn leaves both of them useful to you. A move that scores now but leaves two ends your hand cannot match is often worse than a smaller score that keeps options. The line never branches to rescue you, so the choice among your few legal tiles comes down to the two totals each play leaves behind.
