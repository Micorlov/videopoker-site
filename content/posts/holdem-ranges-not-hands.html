At the table the natural question is “what does he have?”, and the natural answer is a single hand, usually the one that scares you most. The better question is which hands he could have, and how many of each. That set is a range, and because a range is made of countable cards, you can do arithmetic on it.

Combinations: the unit that matters

There are 52 × 51 ÷ 2 = 1,326 distinct two-card starting hands. Many play identically, so they collapse into 169 shapes: 13 pairs, 78 suited combinations of two different ranks and 78 offsuit ones. The post on the 1,326 starting hands derives them.

A pair has 6 combinations because you pick 2 of the 4 suits. A suited hand has 4, one per suit. An offsuit hand has 4 × 3 = 12. So 78 + 312 + 936 = 1,326, and ace-king offsuit is three times as likely to be dealt as ace-king suited. A range’s weight is a count of combinations, not a list of names. The suited-or-offsuit post covers what the matching suit is worth.

An opening range, counted

Say an opponent opens the top 15% of hands. Fifteen per cent of 1,326 is 198.9, so about 199 combinations. Take a list that lands close. Every pair from 22 up is 13 shapes and 78 combinations. Add 18 suited shapes (AKs down to A9s, A8s, A5s, KQs to K9s, QJs to Q9s, JTs, J9s, T9s and 98s) at 4 each for 72, and four offsuit shapes (AKo, AQo, AJo, KQo) at 12 each for 48. That is 35 shapes and 78 + 72 + 48 = 198 combinations, 14.9% of the deck.

Against a flop

Now deal a flop of J♠ 9♠ 4♦. Three cards leave the deck, so any combination using them disappears and the range falls from 198 to 176. Sort those 176 by what they have made, counting a made hand before a draw. A draw here means four to a flush, or four cards inside a five-rank straight window, open-ended or gutshot.

  • Top pair or better: 51. That is 9 sets (JJ, 99 and 44, 3 each), 18 overpairs (AA, KK and QQ, 6 each) and 24 top pairs or two pairs, such as AJs, KJs and J9s.
  • A weaker pair: 57. 42 underpairs (TT, 88, 77, 66, 55, 33 and 22, 6 each) and 15 middle pairs, such as the nines in A9s and 98s.
  • A draw: 29. The suited hands with two spades, plus hands such as KQs and QTs that hold four cards to a straight.
  • Nothing: 39. Hands that missed, such as AKo, AQo, A8s and A5s without spades.

51 + 57 + 29 + 39 = 176. About three in ten are top pair or better, and a little over a fifth have nothing. That beats “he opened, so he has a big hand”, and every number can be checked by listing the combinations.

Blockers: your cards are not in their hand

You hold two of the 52 cards, so they cannot. This is removal, or blockers. Suppose you hold A♠ K♦. Opponents can no longer have ace-ace in 6 ways, only 3, because one ace is yours. The same goes for king-king. Ace-king itself drops from 16 combinations (4 suited, 12 offsuit) to 3 × 3 = 9.

Rerun the flop count with your cards removed and the 176 becomes 146. Top pair or better falls from 51 to 41, because AA and KK lose 3 combinations each. Weaker pairs barely move, 57 to 56. The draws fall from 29 to 19, because your A♠ sits in the spade flush draws, and nothing falls from 39 to 30. Those four add back to 146.

“He could have anything” is a range too

With no information, the honest range is the whole deck: all 1,326 combinations. That still tells you something. 936 of them, 70.6%, are offsuit non-pairs, and only 78 are pairs. A very wide range is mostly weak and misses most boards, so a decent hand is worth betting and a marginal one is worth calling down. “Anything” is mostly nothing.

Against a tight player the strong combinations are a larger share of a smaller range, so their bets deserve more respect.

Narrowing street by street

A range is not fixed. Each action cuts it. Suppose this opponent bets the flop and you assume they only bet with top pair or better and with draws. That keeps 51 + 29 = 80 of the 176, so one bet removes more than half the range. A second bet on a blank turn leaves the same 80, because a draw is still a reason to bet.

On the river the draws that missed have nothing, so a third bet is a made hand from the 51 or a bluff from those 29. If they bluff with every missed draw, the bluffs are 29 of 80, 36%. If they bluff with none, you face 51 made hands. Which you think likelier is the real decision, and you can only make it because you counted.

The same counting sits behind continuation bets.

Count, don’t guess: a pair is 6 combinations, a suited hand 4, an offsuit hand 12, and 1,326 in all. An opening range of 198 combinations became 176 on a flop and 146 after your own two cards came out. Each bet narrows what is left.
