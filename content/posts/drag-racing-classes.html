Drag racing is often described as a straight contest of speed, and at the top level it is. Lower down, most of the racing is set up so that cars of very different performance can meet on equal terms. Two ideas run through the whole sport: classes that group similar cars together, and brackets that let dissimilar ones race anyway. This post sets out how each works, with the figures for the fastest classes and a worked example of a handicap start.

Heads-up racing: the pro classes

In a heads-up class both cars leave on the same starting signal and the first across the line wins, provided it has not fouled. Success comes down to reaction time, elapsed time and, in a close race, trap speed, all of which a time slip records.

Top Fuel is the quickest class. Its dragsters run about 3.6 to 3.7 seconds at over 330 mph. Since 2008 the NHRA has run Top Fuel and Funny Car over 1,000 feet rather than a quarter mile, so those times are for 304.8 metres, not 402.3. Pro Stock cars, which look far more like road cars, run the full quarter mile in about 6.5 seconds at roughly 210 mph.

The two are not directly comparable, because the distances differ. A quick check on the average speeds: 304.8 m in 3.7 s is about 82 m/s, or 184 mph, while 402.3 m in 6.5 s is about 62 m/s, or 138 mph. Both are averages over the whole run from a standing start, so they sit well below the speeds at the finish line.

Bracket racing: the dial-in

Most club and weekend drag racing is bracket racing. Before a round, each driver states a dial-in: the elapsed time they expect to run. The two dial-ins are compared and the car with the slower one leaves first, by exactly the difference between them. If both cars then run their dial-ins, they arrive at the finish line together.

Take the invented example in the chart. Car A is dialled in at 10.50 s and car B at 12.00 s, both over a quarter mile. The difference is 12.00 − 10.50 = 1.50 s, so car B leaves 1.50 s before car A. If each runs exactly its dial-in, B finishes at 12.00 s after its own start, and A, starting 1.50 s later, finishes at 1.50 + 10.50 = 12.00 s after B's start. A dead heat. In practice the winner is whichever driver reacts and runs closer to the number, and the quicker car has no built-in advantage.

The breakout rule: in most bracket classes, a driver who runs quicker than their own dial-in has “broken out” and loses, even if they cross the line first. Under the usual rules, if both break out, the one who broke out by less is generally the winner, and a red-light foul is judged before the breakout.

The breakout rule is what stops drivers from dialling in slower than they can run, a practice called sandbagging. A car that really runs 10.50 but is dialled in at 11.00 would break out on every pass, so the safe strategy is an honest, precise dial-in and a repeatable run. That is why consistent, well-prepared cars do well in brackets. The skill is in running the same number each time, not in running the quickest one.

Classes within a bracket day

Sportsman and bracket events also group cars. Entries are commonly split by type or by how quick the car is, so a day might have a class for street-legal cars and another for purpose-built ones. The details vary from track to track and from sanctioning body to sanctioning body, so check the rules for the event you enter. The principle is the same everywhere: a class is a way to decide who races whom, and the bracket system then evens out the rest.
