You are dealt 10-10-A-K-Q and decide to keep the tens and exchange the other three cards. How often does that turn the pair into something better? About 29% of the time. Roughly seven exchanges in ten leave you holding exactly the pair you started with, only now with three different cards beside it.

That figure is not a rule of thumb. It comes from counting every possible draw, and the count is short enough to check by hand.

The 16,215 possible draws

You can see five cards, so 47 are unseen: the deck minus your hand. With a pair of tens held, two tens remain among them, and the other 45 cards are everything else. Three cards come from those 47, order ignored, so there are 47 × 46 × 45 ÷ 6 = 16,215 equally likely draws. Each outcome below is a count of those draws divided by 16,215.

The rank of your pair makes no difference: two cards of it are always left, and the other ranks keep three or four each.

Checking two of the rows yourself

Four of a kind is the easiest. You need both remaining tens, and then any third card from the other 45. There is one way to take both tens and 45 ways to pick the third, so 45 draws, and 45 ÷ 16,215 = 0.28%.

A full house comes two ways. Either you draw a pair of one new rank plus one ten, or you draw three of a kind of a new rank. For the first, there are 63 possible pairs of a rank other than tens (nine ranks with four cards left give six pairs each, and three ranks with three cards left, the ranks of the cards you threw away, give three pairs each). Multiply by the two tens you could pair with, and you get 126. For the second, nine ranks give four triples each and the three discarded ranks give one each: 39. In total 126 + 39 = 165 draws, or 1.02%.

The four improvements add up: 2,592 + 1,854 + 165 + 45 = 4,656, and 4,656 ÷ 16,215 = 28.71%.

Why a kicker costs you

Some players keep a high card beside the pair, holding A-10-10 and exchanging two cards, on the idea that an ace helps when hands are compared. The count shows what that choice costs in improvement chance. With two cards to come from the same 47 there are 47 × 46 ÷ 2 = 1,081 draws, and these improve the hand:

That is 25.90% against 28.71%, a loss of 2.81 points. The kicker does raise the chance of two pair (17.21% against 15.99%), because your ace now has three partners left to pair with. But you give up a third card, and trips fall from 11.43% to 7.77%. The mistake is the same one the video poker post Why a kicker never helps works through, where the machine pays on a fixed chart. Against an opponent a kicker can break a tie between equal pairs, so it is not worthless, but it is not free.

Drawing three to a pair improves it 28.71% of the time. Keep a kicker and draw two, and the figure drops to 25.90%.

What an improvement is worth

Of the 4,656 improving draws, 2,592 end in two pair and 1,854 in three of a kind. Those two results are 95.5% of the improvements, so in practice "improving" means one of those two. A full house or four of a kind is rare: 210 draws out of 16,215, or 1.30% together.

It helps to know where those results sit in the ranking. In How rare each five-card poker hand is, the full count of dealt hands shows how common a pair is (the most common made hand) and how much rarer two pair and trips are than one pair.

Using the numbers at the table

  1. Expect to miss. A missed draw is the usual outcome, not bad luck.
  2. Count unseen cards, not the full deck. With a pair held there are always 47 unseen cards at the start of the draw, and exactly two of them match your pair.
  3. Price the extra card. Keeping a fifth card costs about 2.8 points of improvement chance. Keep one only if you have a reason.
