A check-raise is two actions in one betting round. You check when the action reaches you, an opponent behind you bets, and the action comes back round to you: you raise their bet instead of calling or folding. It needs someone to act after you, which is why only the player out of position can do it. On the flop the first player to act checks; if the player in position bets, the first player has the chance to raise.

The line, with numbers

Take a pot of 100 on the flop. You act first and check. The player in position bets 60, so the pot is 160. You raise to 200, which means 200 of your own chips go in, since you have put nothing in yet this street.

  • Pot after your raise: 160 + 200 = 360.
  • Their price to call: 200 − 60 = 140 more, to win that 360.
  • Equity they need: 140 ÷ (360 + 140) = 140 ÷ 500 = 28%.

That is the same division as in the post on pot odds, applied to their side of the table. A hand with less than that has to fold or call at a loss.

What the raise has to achieve as a bluff

Suppose your hand has no chance of winning at showdown and you raise purely to win the pot. If they fold, you win 160, the pot you took. If they call, you lose your 200. The fold frequency f that makes those equal is the same formula used in the post on continuation bets:

f = 200 ÷ (200 + 160) = 200 ÷ 360 = 55.6%.

Check it over nine tries. They fold five times and you win 5 × 160 = 800. They call four times and you lose 4 × 200 = 800. The total is zero.

It is tempting to use the 140 figure here, 140 ÷ (140 + 160) = 46.7%, because 140 is what the opponent adds. That is the wrong risk for a bluff. You were not going to call the 60 with a hand that cannot win, so the whole 200 is at stake, not the extra 140.

A pure bluff is not the only reason to raise. Give the hand a flush draw, which from the flop to the river hits 35.0% of the time. If you are called and the pot of 500 is played out with no more bets, your share is 0.35 × 500 = 175 against the 200 you put in, a loss of only 25. Now the break-even is f × 160 = (1 − f) × 25, so f = 25 ÷ 185 = 13.5%. That figure is generous, because it ignores later bets, which usually go against a drawing hand, but it shows the shape: a draw needs far fewer folds than a pure bluff.

Why not just lead, or just call?

You have three ways to play a strong hand out of position. Compare them in the same 100 pot.

  1. Lead. Bet 60 yourself. The player in position now chooses: fold, call, or raise. If you are called, the pot is 220.
  2. Check, then call. Check, they bet 60, you call. The pot is also 220, and you have given up the chance to build it.
  3. Check-raise. Check, they bet 60, you raise to 200 and they call. The pot is 500, more than twice the size.

The check-raise gets there because they volunteered the first bet. A lead bet, sometimes called a donk bet, lets the opponent decide cheaply whether to fold, while a check invites a bet you can then raise.

Strong hands and draws together

If you only check-raise with the best hands, an opponent learns to fold their medium hands every time and never pays you. If you only check-raise as a bluff, they call all the time. A raise works when it looks the same whatever you hold, so a sound range mixes strong made hands, which want the money in, with draws and the occasional bluff, which have fold equity and outs. The strong hands earn the value; the draws make the raise believable and keep it profitable when called.

Where it works less well

  • Against a player who checks back a lot. The whole line depends on them betting. If they check behind often, you rarely get the chance, and when they do bet it is with a stronger selection than usual. The raise then runs into hands that call, and fold equity falls below the 55.6% a bluff needs.
  • Multiway. Every extra opponent must fold for a bluff to work, so check-raise bluffs belong in heads-up pots.

The position rule

The mechanics give a rule. You can only check-raise if someone acts after you has the chance to bet. On the button you act last on the flop, so a check from you ends the round: nobody is left to bet. That means you can never check-raise from the button on the flop. The post on why position matters covers the rest of the trade.

The rule in one line: after a bet of 60 into 100, a raise to 200 costs the opponent 140 for a 360 pot, so they need 28%, and a pure bluff needs 200 ÷ 360 = 55.6% folds; keep strong hands and draws in the mix.
