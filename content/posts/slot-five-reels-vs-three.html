A ways-to-win game counts every left-to-right path across the screen, one cell per reel. The earlier posts on what 243 ways means and on why wins read from the left cover how a win is counted. This one is about the reel count itself, and why it matters so much more than it looks.

Multiply, do not add

With three rows, each reel offers three cells, and a path picks exactly one of them. So every reel you add multiplies the number of paths by 3:

  1. 1 reel: 3 ways.
  2. 2 reels: 3 × 3 = 9 ways.
  3. 3 reels: 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 ways.
  4. 4 reels: 27 × 3 = 81 ways.
  5. 5 reels: 81 × 3 = 243 ways.
  6. 6 reels: 243 × 3 = 729 ways.
  7. 7 reels: 729 × 3 = 2,187 ways.

Compare that with the screen. Three reels show 3 × 3 = 9 cells and five reels show 5 × 3 = 15, so the screen is only six cells bigger, or about one and two-thirds times the size. The number of ways, though, goes from 27 to 243, which is nine times as many: two extra reels, and two extra factors of 3, since 3 × 3 = 9. Cells grow by addition. Ways grow by repeated multiplication, which is what an exponential is.

On a log scale, steady multiplication shows up as a staircase of equal steps. Each bar in the chart is the same length longer than the last, and that regularity is the signature of exponential growth. A straight rise on an ordinary scale would show 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 instead.

What it does to how often you win

A run of matching symbols has to reach a minimum length before it pays. On a three-reel game that minimum is the whole screen: a win means a match on reel 1, reel 2 and reel 3, with nothing left over. On a five-reel game a run of three is already a win, and the two reels that follow can add more.

Here is an illustration with made-up odds, not any real game. Suppose a given symbol shows somewhere on a reel one time in four. The chance of it covering the first three reels is 1/4 × 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/64. On three reels that is the only kind of win available. On five reels, 1/64 is merely the entry ticket. Reaching a fourth reel is 1/4 × 1/64 = 1/256, and a fifth is 1/4 × 1/256 = 1/1024.

That gives a five-reel game a ladder of three rungs, each about four times rarer than the one below. The common rung can pay a little and the rare rungs can pay a lot, and every one of them is still counted across all 243 paths. A three-reel game has one rung. To make wins land often it has to make its winning symbols common, which pushes every prize down together. Five reels let a designer separate the frequent, small win from the rare, large one. The same maths is why the ways count matters. With more paths, a single screen has more chances to contain a run, so the three-reel entry ticket on a five-reel game turns up with a count of paths attached to it. Why each win has to be small

More ways does not mean a bigger return. What a game gives back overall is set by the pay table together with the symbols on each reel, a subject covered in reel strips and stops. If 243 paths find wins more often than 27 do, the pay for each one has to be lower to keep the overall return where it was.

So a ways slot on five reels tends to produce many small wins, plenty of them worth less than the bet, and leaves the large amounts to the long runs and the bonus features. This is a design trade, not a flaw: the screen is busier and pays more often, but any single payment is modest.

Why five became the standard

Three reels was the shape of the classic mechanical machine, and it suited the hardware. Video screens removed that limit, and five reels settled in as the sweet spot for three reasons that you can check against the figures above.

  1. Enough paths. 243 is a large count, but it is not out of reach. With four reels the figure is 81, which gives fewer routes to a win and a ladder of just two rungs, runs of three and four.
  2. A full ladder. Runs of three, four and five reels give three distinct prize levels, so frequent small wins and rare big ones can sit on the same screen.
  3. Diminishing returns beyond. A sixth reel triples the paths to 729, but on the same one-in-four illustration a six-reel run is 1/4 × 1/1024 = 1/4096. The new top rung is so rare that most players would almost never see it, while the screen gets wider and harder to read at a glance.

Five reels by three rows gives a screen that fits comfortably in landscape, shows 15 symbols at a glance, and carries enough exponential growth to support a full range of wins.
