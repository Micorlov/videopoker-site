Fives and Threes Dominoes keeps a running figure for your average score per hand, alongside matches won, best placement and points per placement. A number on its own does not say whether you are playing well. This post builds a yardstick from the scoring table, shows which part of it is fixed by the rules and which part is yours, and explains why a high average does not always win a match. The scoring itself is covered in every scoring end total, 3 to 20; here that table is used for a different job.

What a scoring placement pays

Between 3 and 20 there are 18 possible open-end totals. Nine divide by three or five and score: 3 pays 1, 5 pays 1, 6 pays 2, 9 pays 3, 10 pays 2, 12 pays 4, 15 pays 8 (the five and the three together), 18 pays 6 and 20 pays 4. The other nine pay nothing, and one of those, 19, cannot be made at all.

The nine scoring totals pay 31 points between them, so the mean of a scoring total is 31 ÷ 9, about 3.44. That treats every scoring total as equally likely, which they are not, so count the ways instead. A pair of open-end faces is a plain end (0 to 6 spots) or a double at an end (0 to 12 spots, counting both halves), and a double-six cannot sit at both ends because there is only one. With a standard double-six set that gives 98 possible pairs, and 48 of them make a scoring total. Weighted by those counts the 48 scoring pairs pay 133 points in all, so a scoring pair averages 133 ÷ 48, about 2.77, and a pair picked blindly pays 133 ÷ 98, about 1.36 points.

Two structural facts come out of this. About half of all pairs of ends score (48 of 98, or 49%), so a placement chosen without looking would score roughly one time in two. And the typical scoring placement is small, under 3 points. The big totals, 15, 18 and 20, are the ones that need a double at an end, and they are rare as pairs: 15 comes from only two pairs and 20 from one.

From a placement to a hand

A hand gives you a fixed number of tiles, so a fixed number of placements, give or take the tiles you draw. The yardstick is then one product: placements, times the chance a placement scores, times the average scoring value. With the blind-pair figures above, the first two factors and the average fold into 133 ÷ 98 points per placement.

  • 6 tiles: about 8.1 points per hand
  • 7 tiles: about 9.5
  • 8 tiles: about 10.9
  • 9 tiles: about 12.2

This is the benchmark for a player who plays every tile without choosing, in a hand where all the tiles get played. Real hands differ in three ways. Drawing from the boneyard adds placements, and a blocked line removes them. Ends are not random, because a tile must match the end it joins. And you choose, which is the point of the game.

The choice is the part that belongs to you. A player who always picks the highest-scoring legal tile raises the chance that a placement scores above 49%, and lifts the average scoring value above 2.77. Neither lift has a fixed size; it depends on the tiles dealt, which is why this post gives no target for a strong player. A sensible reading is that an average per hand near the benchmark for your hand size means your choices are doing little more than chance, and one clearly above it means you are finding scoring totals and 15s that blind play would miss.

If you want to compare your own statistics, the settings must match. Fives and Threes Dominoes lets you start with 6, 7, 8 or 9 tiles each and switch the going-out bonus on or off, and its Statistics screen shows your average score per hand.

Why the same player averages differently

Hand size is the clearest cause. The table above climbs from 8.1 to 12.2 on size alone, with no change in skill, so an average from 9-tile hands and one from 6-tile hands cannot be compared. The going-out bonus is a switch the app lets you turn on or off, so the same play can produce a different average with it on. The listing does not give the bonus amount, so do not assume the size of that shift. Compare only hands played with the same hand size and bonus setting. Difficulty is a setting too, so keep that the same, and note that the match target changes how you play, which is the next point. When a high average loses the match

A match is won by reaching the target first, which can be 61, 121, 181 or 241 points, not by scoring the most per hand. At 9.5 a hand, the benchmark above, a player reaches 61 in roughly six or seven hands on paper. But hands are shared with an opponent, and the one who goes out or blocks first ends the hand while the other still holds tiles. Near the finish, a placement that scores 2 and leaves the opponent no scoring reply can be worth more than a 4 that hands them a 15.

So chasing the highest average per hand is a mistake late in a short match. Early on, build points; once the opponent is close to 61, count the ends you leave. The tactics for that are in playing to 61, and the logic of shutting a line down is in blocking. A modest average that wins matches beats a high one that does not.
