A steam locomotive is a boiler on wheels with an engine bolted to the side. Nothing in it is mysterious, and the numbers are small enough to do by hand. This post follows the energy from the shovel to the rail, then puts a figure on the pull at the end.

From coal to steam

Coal burns on a grate in the firebox. The hot gases do not go straight up the chimney. They are drawn forward through the boiler in many narrow fire tubes, which run through the water. Many tubes give the gases a far larger area to give up their heat than a single wide flue would.

The water boils and the steam collects in a dome at the top of the boiler, under pressure. Typical working pressures for later steam locomotives were around 200 psi (about 14 bar), and some ran higher. Pressure is the useful part: it is what pushes on the piston.

Many engines then pass the steam through a superheater, a bank of pipes inside the larger fire tubes. Superheating raises the steam well above its boiling point at that pressure, so it is dry. Dry steam does not condense inside the cylinders, where water would waste heat and could damage the engine, and it gives more work from each kilogram of water.

Cylinders, rods and wheels

A valve admits steam to a cylinder and pushes the piston along it. The piston rod and connecting rod carry that push to a crank pin on the driving wheel, which turns it. The wheel's own rotation brings the piston back again.

Railway cylinders are double-acting. Steam is let in on one side of the piston for the push out, and on the other side for the push back. That gives two power strokes per cylinder for every turn of the wheel. A conventional locomotive has two cylinders, one on each side, with their cranks set a quarter of a turn apart so the engine can always start from rest. So:

2 cylinders × 2 strokes each = 4 power strokes per wheel revolution

This is the origin of the four chuffs per wheel turn heard from a two-cylinder engine. At a steady pace of one turn of the driving wheels per second, that is four chuffs a second.

The exhaust that feeds the fire

After each stroke the used steam leaves the cylinder and is sent up the chimney through a narrow blastpipe. The jet of exhaust pulls gas out of the smokebox with it, which lowers the pressure there and draws air through the fire tubes and in through the firebox grate. This is the sharp bark of the exhaust, and it has a useful feature: the harder the engine works, the more steam it exhausts, and the harder the fire is drawn. The draught rises with the demand, without anyone touching a control.

How much can it pull?

The standard rule of thumb for a two-cylinder locomotive is:

tractive effort = 0.85 × P × d² × s ÷ D

where P is boiler pressure in psi, d the cylinder diameter in inches, s the piston stroke in inches, and D the driving wheel diameter in inches. The result is in pounds-force (lbf). The 0.85 is a conventional allowance for the cylinders not being filled with full boiler pressure throughout the stroke. It is an estimate, not a measurement.

Take an illustrative engine: 200 psi boiler pressure, two cylinders of 20 in diameter and 26 in stroke, and driving wheels of 68 in.

The formula shows how the design trades force against speed. A bigger cylinder, a longer stroke or more pressure raises the pull. A bigger wheel lowers it, because the same push acts on a longer lever. One turn of a 68 in wheel moves the engine π × 68 in, which is about 5.43 m, so a large wheel covers more ground per stroke and suits fast trains. A small wheel gives more pull and suits heavy freight.

Whether the engine can use all that force is a separate question. As the post on why railways follow rivers explains, adhesion between wheel and rail limits what any locomotive can pull, however hard its cylinders push.

A steam locomotive is a poor converter of energy. Typically less than about 10 per cent of the heat in the coal ends up as work at the wheels. Most leaves as hot gas up the chimney, as exhaust steam and as heat from the boiler.

Why efficiency stays low

The exhaust steam is still hot and still carries away much of the heat that went into the water. A locomotive cannot recover it, because the exhaust is also what makes the draught. Superheating and better valves improved matters, but under 10 per cent remained the usual range.
