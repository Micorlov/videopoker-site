A wagon is not limited by its total weight. It is limited by the weight each axle carries onto the rails, because that is the load the rail, the sleepers and the bridges have to take at every point of contact. The arithmetic is a single division, and it explains why a wagon’s axle count matters as much as its size.

The sum

Axle load is the gross weight of the wagon, which means its own weight plus its load, divided by its number of axles. A four-axle wagon at 90 tonnes gross puts 90 ÷ 4 = 22.5 tonnes on each axle. That sits exactly on the common continental limit.

Now try to carry more. At 100 t gross the same four axles carry 25 t each, which is over the 22.5 t limit but inside Britain’s. At 120 t gross they would carry 30 t each. To bring that back to 22.5 t you need 120 ÷ 22.5 = 5.3 axles, so in practice six, which gives 20 t each. The weight did not become safer; it was spread over more places.

What the routes allow

The limits differ by network, and the spread is large:

  • Standard lines in continental Europe are commonly rated at 22.5 t per axle under the UIC (International Union of Railways) classes.
  • Britain is largely restricted to about 25.4 t per axle, and routes are classified by RA (route availability) numbers that say which vehicles may run.
  • North American freight runs at about 32.5 t per axle. The standard is 286,000 lb gross on four axles, which is about 129.7 t, or about 32.4 t each.
  • The heaviest iron-ore railways go further still, to about 40 t per axle on lines built or rebuilt for the purpose.

What actually sets the limit

Three things push back as the axle load rises.

The rail itself. A wheel presses on a contact patch about the size of a small coin, so the pressure in the steel is very high. It rises with the load, but more slowly than the load does: for a fixed wheel and rail shape, contact pressure goes up roughly with the cube root of the force. The rail also bends between its supports, and that bending stress rises in direct proportion to the wheel load.

The sleepers and ballast. The rail spreads each wheel load over several sleepers, and the sleepers spread it over the ballast and the ground below. A heavier axle presses harder on each of them, so the stones crush and the track settles faster. Wear is worse than proportional: engineers often quote a power of three or four, so a modest rise in axle load shortens track life by a larger fraction.

The bridges. This is usually the binding limit. A bridge is designed for a given load, and a short span carries one or two axles at a time, so a single heavy axle is the case that counts. How railway bridges carry a load shows how a span turns the weight on it into forces in its members.

Because a single weak bridge can restrict an entire line, the limit belongs to the route and not to the vehicle. A wagon is not given a licence; the line is given a rating, and a vehicle may run only where its axle load fits inside it. That is the purpose of Britain’s RA numbers.

Spreading it out

Given a fixed limit, there are two ways to move more freight. The first is more axles under each wagon: a bogie wagon, with pivoting frames holding two or three axles each, spreads 120 t over six axles at 20 t each. The second is more wagons, since the limit applies to each axle and not to the length of the train. Heavier trains also take longer to stop, as why trains can’t stop quickly explains.

The third way is to raise the limit, and that costs money: heavier rail, better sleepers, deeper ballast and stronger bridges along the whole line. Heavy-haul lines pay for it because the tonnage is huge and steady.

A cheap branch has a low ceiling

The same rule works backwards. A branch line laid with light rail, on thin ballast and with a few old bridges, gets a low rating, and it will keep that rating whatever the traffic wants. A cheap route saves money on building and then caps the freight that can run over it, so a mine at the end of the line would need the whole route upgraded to ship more per wagon.

What to take from it

  • Axle load is gross weight divided by axles: 90 t on four axles is 22.5 t each.
  • Routes are rated, not wagons: about 22.5 t on UIC lines, 25.4 t in Britain, 32.5 t in North America and about 40 t on heavy iron-ore lines.
  • Rail stress, sleeper and ballast pressure and above all bridges set the limit.
  • To carry more, add axles or add wagons, or pay to strengthen the whole route.
