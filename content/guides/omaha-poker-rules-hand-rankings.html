Omaha is the second most popular form of community-card poker after Texas Hold'em, and almost always played as Pot-Limit Omaha, or PLO. If you know Hold'em you already know most of the game. This guide covers what is different, and the hand-reading traps that catch players coming over from Hold'em.

How a hand of Omaha is played

  1. Blinds. The two players to the left of the dealer button post a small and a big blind.
  2. The deal. Each player gets four cards face down, their hole cards.
  3. Pre-flop betting. Starting left of the big blind, each player folds, calls or raises.
  4. The flop. Three community cards are dealt face up, then a second betting round starting left of the button.
  5. The turn. A fourth community card, then a third betting round.
  6. The river. A fifth community card, then the last betting round.
  7. Showdown. Remaining players show their cards, and the best five-card hand wins the pot.

The button moves one seat to the left after every hand, so everyone takes turns acting last, which is a large advantage in Omaha (why position matters).

The rule: exactly two from your hand, exactly three from the board

In Hold'em you may use both, one or none of your hole cards. In Omaha you must use exactly two of your four hole cards together with exactly three of the five board cards. No more, no fewer. Nearly every misread hand in Omaha comes from forgetting this. (More examples of the rule.)

Omaha hand rankings

The rankings are the same as every other standard poker game. What changes is how often each hand turns up: with four hole cards, everyone has more combinations, so the winning hand at showdown is usually much stronger than in Hold'em.

Two practical consequences:

  • Play for the nuts. The best possible hand on a given board is called the nuts, and in multi-way Omaha pots someone often has it (reading the nuts). A second-best flush loses far more often than in Hold'em (why).
  • A paired board means full houses are possible, and straights and flushes lose value fast (paired boards).

Pot-limit betting: how big can you bet?

In Pot-Limit Omaha the most you can bet or raise is the size of the pot. For a raise, the pot is counted after you have called. A quick formula for the largest raise: three times the last bet, plus whatever was in the pot before it.

Omaha Hi-Lo (Omaha 8 or better)

In Hi-Lo the pot is split between the best high hand and the best qualifying low hand.

  • A low hand is five different cards ranked 8 or lower. Aces count low, and straights and flushes don't spoil a low. The best low is A-2-3-4-5.
  • The two-plus-three rule still applies, and you may use different hole cards for your high and your low.
  • A low is only possible when the board shows at least three different cards of 8 or lower. If nobody qualifies, the high hand takes the whole pot.

Read Omaha Hi-Lo eight or better, then the two traps: counterfeiting and getting quartered.

Starting hands, in brief

There are 270,725 different four-card starting hands (how that's counted), and they run much closer together than in Hold'em. The strongest share three features:

  • All four cards work together. A hand like J♠ 10♥ 9♠ 8♥ makes straights many ways (rundowns). A card that connects to nothing is a dangler.
  • Suits. Two cards of each of two suits, called double-suited, gives you two flush draws (double-suited hands). Three of one suit is weaker than it looks (why).
  • High cards. Pocket aces are a good start, but much weaker than in Hold'em without support.
